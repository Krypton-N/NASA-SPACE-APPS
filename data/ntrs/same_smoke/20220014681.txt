The ongoing Stratospheric Aerosol and Gas Experiment aboard the International Space Station (SAGE III/ISS) has 
mostly occurred during a period of relatively low aerosol loading. At the same time, it has almost observed a number 
of small to moderate injections of volcanic material in the stratosphere from the Aoba/Ambae eruption of 2018 to 
that of Karymsky in 2022. In addition, two of the largest known smoke intrusions into the stratosphere have occurred 
during the SAGE III/ISS mission (the British Columbia �re of August 2017 and the Australian Wild�re Event of 2019/2020).   
At least two other much smaller smoke events have also been observed in the SAGE III data (California Creek in 
September 2020 and McKay Creek in June 2021).  

Particularly with the two large events, it is tempting to speculate that these events are increasing in intensity and frequen-
cy possibly due to ongoing climate change. Realistically, change is di�cult to assess since instrument data sets that can 
be used for quantifying historical events can be quite di�erent in what and where they provide measurements.   This com-
plicates comparing one event to another potentially separated by many years. An exception to this issues are the mea-
suremens by SAGE III/ISS and those of its predecessor SAGE II (1984-2005). Both use the same measurement strategy, 
measure aerosol extinction coe�cient at 1020 nm, and have similar spatial/temporal coverage. Both instruments are 
known to observe smoke events in the stratosphere. However, both have the same low observation density characteristic 
of solar occultation such that near-global coverage takes almost a month and characterizing 
e�ectively instant events like smoke and volcanic events take a matter of weeks depending 
on the vagaries of where the observations are being made at a critical juncture unlike instru-
ments with high temporal/spatial resolution like limb-scatter instruments such as OMPS and 
OSIRIS. The goal of this work will be to use the same analysis approach to both the SAGE II and 
SAGE III data and compare both the frequency and intensity of smoke events observed by 
these instruments. While we do not expect to be able to make conclusions about change be-
tween the periods, the analysis is a �rst step in interpreting changes in the contribution of 
smoke to the stratospheric aerosol budget between the time frames encompassed by these 
instruments.
