Opposed-flow spreading flames: Effect of sub-atmospheric 
pressure on spread and burning rates 

Luca Carmignani1, Priya Garg2, Michael J. Gollner3, Carlos Fernandez-Pello4 
University of California, Berkeley, Berkeley, CA, 94720, USA 

Maria Thomsen5 
Universidad Adolfo Ibáñez, Santiago, Chile 

and 

David L. Urban6, Gary A. Ruff7 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135, USA 

Flame spread over solid fuels is a canonical problem in fire science, due to its direct 
implications on material flammability and importance in fire development. Flames in a 
microgravity environment can behave very differently than on Earth, posing additional risks 
for spaceflight life safety. Sub-atmospheric pressures in ground-based experiments have been 
proposed to approximately replicate the burning behavior of solid fuels in reduced gravity 
conditions because of similar effects on heat and mass transfer mechanisms from the flame to 
the solid. In opposed-flow flame spread, the solid fuel is heated by the flame ahead of its 
leading edge, and this process is strongly affected by the ambient conditions. In this work, we 
consider flames spreading over flat thin acrylic samples exposed to a forced flow of 20 cm/s, 
and pressures between 30 and 100 kPa. When radiation losses and chemical kinetic effects are 
neglected, experimental and theoretical results suggest an independence of the spread rate on 
pressure. However, the spread rate shows a decreasing trend with lower pressure in the range 
30-60 kPa. The mass burning rate, calculated from the samples weight measured before and 
after the experiments, shows a similar trend. The flame length, on the other hand, shows a 
non-monotonic behavior, reaching a maximum at 60 kPa. Additionally, gas emissions 
measured during the experiments are used to estimate the heat release rate of the spreading 
flames. The comparison of the heat release rate obtained from the measured emissions and 
the estimated mass burning rates, suggests that chemical kinetics is not driving the decrease 
in flame spread observed at low pressures, whereas radiation losses could be responsible in 
analogy to what has been observed in micro and partial gravity studies. These results could 
provide more information to guide future Earth-based material flammability testing for 
spacecraft applications.

I. Introduction 
HE flammability of solid combustible materials determines the fire hazard characteristics of the material and thus 
is important in fire safety1. Measured parameters include the material ease of ignition, the rate of flame spread, 

the heat release rate, and the toxicity of the combustion products. Ambient conditions such as pressure, oxygen 
concentration, gas flow, and gravity level, together with the material thermo-physical properties and scale affect these 
flammability properties, and thus it is of interest to determine these effects. 

Material flammability in sub-atmospheric pressures has application in determining the fire hazard in urban areas 
at high altitudes2-4, aircraft5, and spacecraft6,7. Furthermore, since ambient pressure affects the buoyant characteristics 
of a fire, it has the added application of using pressure modeling to simulate fires at different scales8-10. 

Following a similar approach, low pressure environments have been proposed to study the fire behavior in reduced 
gravity conditions11-15. By decreasing the ambient pressure, flames over solid fuels tend to move away from the 
burning surface, and diffusive processes become more important, in analogy with the effect of reduced buoyancy at 
low gravity levels. In microgravity the buoyant flow field is extremely weak and comparable to diffusive flows, and 
flames may not spread without externally generated flows, such as the low velocity flows generated in spacecraft by 
Environmental Control and Life Support Systems (ECLSS). Spreading flames are generally divided into two main 
categories: concurrent flame spread, where the direction of propagation is the same as that of an oncoming flow field, 
including upward-spreading flames on Earth, and counter-current or opposed-flow flame spread, where the flame 
moves against an incoming flow. Concurrent-flow flame spread is known to be more hazardous than opposed-flow 
flame spread in a terrestrial environment. However, it has been shown that in a low-gravity environment opposed-
flow flames can grow more easily than concurrent spreading flames because of a fresh supply of oxidizer16, and 
sometimes burn under conditions where extinguishment would occur on Earth17-19. Previous experiments suggest that 
reduced pressures ranging between 20-40 kPa may be able to simulate flames in microgravity in terms of flame spread 
rate and flame length13,15, at least for thin combustible materials. However, there are some limitations to the 
comparison between low gravity and low pressure. Numerical studies and scaling analyses have highlighted the 
importance of diffusion processes and radiation losses in reduced pressure environments11,14,20. 

As the flame spreads over the surface of the solid, it leaves behind a burning solid that releases heat depending on 
the physico-chemical characteristics of the process. The resulting heat-release rate (HRR) is an important parameter 
used to characterize the flammability of a material, since together with the flame spread rate they determine the growth 
of a fire. The HRR can be derived from the rate of fuel consumption, which is usually calculated using the mass loss

The primary objective of this work is to provide information about the flammability of materials in spacecraft 
environments, although the results have applications to other reduced pressure conditions. Specifically, we study the 
effect of ambient sub-atmospheric pressure on the flammability of thin solid materials, through their rate of flame 
spread, emissions, and the heat release. We consider the opposed/downward spread of flames over thin acrylic samples 
(polymethyl methacrylate, or PMMA), at ambient pressures between 30 and 100 kPa. The flames are exposed to a 
forced velocity of 20 cm/s in addition to the self-generated buoyant flow. The analysis of the flow velocity controlling 
the flame spread developed by Thomsen et al.15 is expanded to correlate the experimentally measured variation of 
flame characteristics such as spread rate and flame length scales with pressure. Additionally, the gas concentrations 
of the combustion products are used to estimate the HRR of the spreading flames, which is shown to decrease with 
pressure. When compared to the HRR estimated from the average mass loss rate of the fuel, the results suggest that 
chemical kinetics is not a driving factor for these conditions. 

II. Experimental setup 
The experiments were conducted in an apparatus 

previously developed to study the flammability of solid 
combustible materials under varied ambient conditions23. 
The apparatus consists of a laboratory scale combustion 
tunnel that is inserted in a pressure chamber as shown in the 
schematic of Figure 1. The tunnel has a 125 mm square cross 
section and a 600 mm total length. The first 350 mm section 
of the duct serves as a flow straightener, the other 250 mm 
segment of the duct is used as the test section. 

The chamber is provided with a flow system that 
provides constant supply and exhaust of gases to avoid 
vitiation problems. Compressed dry air was supplied through 
critical nozzles (O’Keefe Controls) to the bottom of the duct 
while constantly evacuating to maintain constant pressure 
inside the chamber. The chamber pressure was controlled by 
a high-capacity vacuum pump (Welch DuoSeal 1376), and 
measured with an electronic pressure transducer (Omega 
Engineering, Inc. PX309-015A5V). The tests were 
conducted in air at pressures between 30 and 100 ± 2 kPa. 
The forced flow was adjusted to provide 20 cm/s at the 
respective chamber pressure for all tests. This velocity was selected to be consistent with the flow of air induced by 
the ECLSS inside of a spacecraft. In addition to the forced flow, the flame is exposed to a self-induced buoyant flow 
that varies with pressure. Thus, during the experiments the flame is exposed to a mixed (free and forced) flow 
condition. 

Gas measurements were recorded using a gas analyzer Enerac 700. This device provides real-time concentrations 
of oxygen (O2), nitrogen oxide (NO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), oxides of nitrogen (NOx), and sulphur dioxide (SO2) 
using an electrochemical sensor. Carbon dioxide (CO2), carbon monoxide (CO), and unburnt hydrocarbons (HC) were 
calibrated against propane using a NDIR sensor. The flow rate of the sampling pump was set at 1.5 SLPM, and the 
sampling probe was connected to the exhaust system downstream of the vacuum pump (see schematics in Figure 1). 

The fuel samples were thin sheets of cast PMMA, with a thickness of 0.7 mm. The rectangular samples were held 
between two identical stainless-steel frames 0.6 mm thick, with a rectangular opening of 150 mm long by 50 mm wide 
that served as the test area. Each sample was placed vertically at the mid-plane of the test section, and they were 
ignited at the top with a 26-gauge Kanthal wire. The igniter was energized using a controlled current power supply 
(BK Precision 1785) set to deliver 50 W for about 10 s, or until the flame appears on both sides of the sample. The 
flames were extinguished by flushing the system with nitrogen after they had spread over the entire sample region. 
The experiments were repeated three to six times for each test condition to address experimental uncertainty. 

The ignition and subsequent flame spread were video recorded with a resolution of 1920 by 1080 at 30 frames per 
second using a Sony RX10-III camera for the front view, and a second Nikon D3200 camera for the flame side view. 
The camera settings were kept constant during the experiments, although they slightly differ between front view and

The concentration of the post-combustion products of a flame is an important indicator of the completeness of the 
reactions involved in the flame spread. The average O2, CO and CO2 concentrations measured during the flame spread 
experiments were used to estimate the HRR32, and the results are shown in Figure 8. The HRR can also be estimated 
from the mass burning rate of the solid fuel. Since the mass of the samples was measured before and after the 
experiments, we calculated an average mass loss rate; by assuming complete combustion of the solid fuel, we can 
obtain the HRR from the mass measurements simply multiplying the mass loss rate by the heat of combustion of 
PMMA (25.9 kJ/g). The estimated values, reported in Figure 8 as well, follow the same pressure trend of the HRR 
calculated from the emission measurements, as well as the flame spread rate, indicating that they are related to the 
amount of fuel burned as the flame spreads along the solid surface. The calculated values of the HRR from the gas 
composition and mass loss are similar, suggesting that the assumption of complete combustion gives reasonable values 
for the pressure range 30-100 kPa. Furthermore, this implies that the decrease in spread rate is not driven by the onset 
of finite rate kinetics effects; more likely, the flames are weaken by radiative losses, in analogy to what happens in a 
reduced gravity environment. 

V. Conclusion 
Experiments of opposed/downward spreading flames over thin acrylic sheets were conducted at sub-atmospheric 

pressures with the objective of studying the flammability of solid combustible materials in low pressure environments 
such as high-altitude locations, aircraft, and spacecraft. Several flame characteristics are measured from the video 
analysis of the experiments. It is found that the flame spread rate is weakly dependent on pressure in the range 60-100 
kPa, and then it decreases as the ambient pressure is reduced. The pyrolysis and preheating length on the solid surface 
are inversely proportional to pressure, whereas the flame length follows a non-monotonic behavior with a peak at 60 
kPa. The gas concentrations in the post-combustion products were used to calculate the HRR of the spreading flame, 
and the results show a similar trend with the spread rate: it is almost constant in the range 50-100 kPa and then 
decreases at lower pressures. The comparison between the HRR calculated from the gas emissions, and the HRR 
estimated from the mass loss rate suggests that chemical kinetics is not a limiting factor for the spreading flames 
considered in this work, although it seems to affect the efficiency of the gas phase fuel combustion process. It is 
possible that the flow velocity of 20 cm/s used in this work was large enough to guarantee an adequate mixing of the 
reactancts, decreasing the effect of pressure on the chemical reaction rate. The spread of the flames could also be 
weakened by radiative losses, which were neglected in the flame spread rate model. The increase in concavity of the 
burning front of the solid fuel observed at low pressures, in fact, could be caused by heat losses occurring by the 
sample sides. In summary, the reduced ambient pressure on opposed-flow flame spread over thin PMMA samples had 
similar implications to a reduced gravity level, where chemical kinetics is not important and the process is limited by 
flame radiative losses. In order to better compare opposed flame spread in reduced pressure and gravity environments, 
it will be necessary to further investigate and characterize the importance of radiation losses.
