The spread of flames over the surface of solid combustible materials is affected by the 

environmental conditions. Variables such as flow velocity, oxygen concentration, ambient 

pressure, partial gravity or microgravity, may change the material flammability and influence 

the fire dynamics. This is an important fire safety issue for space exploration vehicles and 

space habitats which will very likely have different environments than those currently used 

on the International Space Station, such as reduced pressure and enriched oxygen 

concentration (SEA). However, testing experimentally the materials to be used and qualified 

for space exploration under these conditions is a difficult and expensive task. Here, numerical 

modeling is used to understand the dominant physico-chemical processes on the concurrent 

flame spread over thin fabrics under reduced ambient pressure (and in turn, buoyancy) and 

variable gravitational conditions. The numerical model uses the Fire Dynamics Simulator 

(FDS6) code with a single-step Arrhenius reaction rate for the solid phase decomposition. 

Different models are tested for the gas phase combustion kinetics. The model results are 

validated with experimental results obtained at similar reduced ambient pressure and flow 

conditions at 1 g. It is shown that as ambient pressure is reduced the flame spread rate over a 

thin fabric is also reduced, both experimentally and numerically. Numerical results are 

compared to an analytical approach previously developed to explain the experimental trends. 

Further interpretation of the model results provides information regarding the physics of the 

process and how they are affected by the lower pressure environments and gravity conditions. 

The results of this work provide guidance for potential on-earth testing for fire safety design 

in spacecraft and space habitats.

to determine the flammability of solid combustible materials [1,2]. A flame spreads over a solid fuel surface by first 

preheating the virgin fuel to its pyrolysis, pyrolyzing it, and igniting the resulting flammable mixture of pyrolyzate 

and oxygen. Thus, the flame propagates in a creeping fashion as a series of ignition steps where the flame acts both 

as the source of heating and flammable gas ignition [1–3]. Thus, the spread of flames over the surface of a solid is a 

complex phenomenon resulting from a number of processes occurring in both the solid phase (heat transfer, thermal 

decomposition, gasification) and the gas phase (transport, mixing, combustion) which are very much affected by 

external environmental conditions such as oxidizer flow velocity, pressure, oxygen concentration, buoyancy (gravity 

level) or external heating. Thus, there is an interest in studying the effect of environmental conditions on flame spread.  

Concurrent, or flow assisted, flame spread occurs when the spread of the flame and the gas flow are in the same 

direction (upward spread in buoyant flow). Because the gas flow assists the transfer of heat from the flame to the solid 

and consequently the spread of the flame, this mode of flame spread is generally fast and consequently hazardous. It 

is for this reason that some material flammability tests use this mode of flame spread to characterize the fire hazard of 

combustible materials. In this flame spread configuration, the solid preheating and pyrolysis rates are dominated by 

convection and flame radiation, and the external flow velocity will therefore influence both the flame length and the 

heat flux from the flame to the solid surface [4–8]. Since there are marked differences in the velocity distributions in 

flame induced buoyant flows at different pressure [9] and gravity levels [10], it is expected that there will be also 

notable differences in the spread of flames. The ambient pressure affects, through the density, the thickness of the 

boundary layer, and consequently the position of the flame respect to the solid surface (Fig. 1). As the pressure is 

reduced the boundary layer thickens, the flame moves away from the surface, and the heat transferred from the flame 

to the solid surface is reduced. Consequently, the rate of flame spread is reduced, both because the reduced heat flux 

on the surface and a reduction in the flame length [11]. Thus, the effect on the flame characteristics and the spread 

rate, of reducing the ambient pressure is similar to that of reducing the flow velocity since it also increases the 

boundary layer thickness and in turn the flame stand-off distance. This is relevant because in a high-altitude location 

or in an aircraft for example, the ambient pressure will be lower than at sea level. In the case of a spacecraft such as 

the International Space Station the cabin environment may be quite different, although the ambient pressure is 

approximately that of standard Earth, it includes microgravity, low velocity flows induced by the spacecraft 

ventilation. This low velocity flows encountered in a spacecraft are on the order of ~10–20 cm/s and cannot be easily 

replicated on earth because the buoyant flow (~40 cm/s) dominates over the forced flow, making flammability testing 

of materials in spacecraft conditions even harder. 

 

 
Several studies have been conducted on the spread of flames over solid fuels under reduced pressure to observe 

the effect of buoyancy on flame spread in reduced gravity [12–16], flame spread in a high-altitude location [17–19], 

and limiting condition for flame spread [20,21]. However, there is still a need to systematically study and understand 

the effect of pressure, and consequently buoyancy, on the spread of flame over different solid combustible materials. 

In this regard, the development of numerical models that can facilitate the understanding of the processes involved 

and guide experimental research on earth is a very important useful tool. Theoretical and numerical analyses that

describe the flame spread behavior over solid fuels must handle the complexity of the entire process by coupling the 

gas phase and solid phase reactions involved. Computational fluid dynamic models usually rely in representative 

thermochemical and kinetic parameters, as well as material properties that are not readily available for most 

engineering materials. Nevertheless, despite the restrictions and complications, numerical models are actively being 

used to complement experimental work. Several models have been developed to simulate burning of thermally thin 

[10,22–26] and thick solids [27–29]. Most of these previous works have used one-step global reactions for the gas

thermal decomposition models [25,26,28–30] to some more complex pyrolysis models that include the formation of 

char or other intermediate components [24,31–33].  

The overall objective of this work is to determine the effect of ambient pressure, and consequently buoyancy, on 

the upward/concurrent flame spread process over a thin cotton fabric in low velocity air flows through numerical 

simulations. The material has been selected because it is common clothing material, and also it will be one of the 

materials that will be tested in the microgravity experiments to be conducted during NASA's Spacecraft Fire 

Experiment (Saffire), on board of the Orbital Corporation Cygnus spacecraft [14]. The range of ambient pressures and 

reduced gravity levels has been selected to cover a wide range of environments and buoyantly induced gas flow 

velocities. Based on previous analytical models [15,34], we hypothesize that transport processes are dominant in 

determining the upward/concurrent flame spread rates on thin materials. The specific objective of the research 

presented is to compare numerical simulations to previous analytical models, improving the current understanding of 

the causes behind the decreasing flame spread rate under reduced buoyant flows (i.e. gravity and pressure). Through 

different numerical simulations, this analysis will help elucidate the relative importance between hydrodynamics, heat 

transport or chemical kinetics, and under what conditions certain processes will prevail over others. 

II. Model Description 

The numerical model is a two-dimensional simulation of the experiment done using the Fire Dynamics Simulator 

(FDS) [35] version 6.7.0. The computational domain is 100 mm in the z-direction and 60 mm in the x-direction, as 

shown in Fig. 2. A grid sensitivity analysis was carried for cells with sizes varying from 0.8 to 0.25 mm in both 

directions. This grid dependence analysis was performed at the highest gravity (1g – earth gravity) and ambient 

pressure (100 kPa) considered as part of this study, where the gas reaction and the buoyancy effects on the flow are 

expected to be strongest. A cell size of 0.4 mm in the z-direction and the x-direction was chosen to be used for the 

solid and the gas phase in all simulations, providing adequate results for the range of pressure analyzed (changes to a 

smaller grid varied the predicted flame spread rate less than 2% from the one obtained at the selected grid value). This 

cell size was obtained from uniformly dividing the total domain in 250 cells in the z-direction and 150 cells in the x-

direction. Following the findings of Fereres et al. [28] that the level of grid refinement necessary for grid insensitivity 

varies with ambient pressure, requiring larger grid sizes at lower ambient pressure, the same grid size was used for all 

the conditions tested.  
The +z and the x+ domain were established as an outlet with an “open” boundary condition. This “open” condition 

is defined by FDS as a passive opening on the exterior boundary of the computational field that allows flow into or 

out of the domain depending on the local pressure gradient [35]. An inlet boundary condition was defined on the -z of 

the domain to give a constant forced flow of 20 cm/s, similar to the forced flow used in the experiments. The inlet 

flow is a nitrogen/oxygen mixture with the oxygen concentration of normal air (21% by vol.) at a prescribed ambient 

temperature (20 °C) and pressure; the ambient pressure is varied depending on the test being simulated. 

The sample is modeled as a thin vertical solid located on the -x coordinate of the domain and represented by an 

obstruction of 2 cell thick. The fuel used is cotton and the sample thickness is 0.3 mm (value considered for all heat 

transfer calculations).  The model is set to burn the material in an upward configuration of flame spread so, during the 

simulations, gravity is set in the -z direction and its value modified from terrestrial 9.81 m/s2 to microgravity. An 

igniter is modeled as a solid block 1 cell thick by 3 cell long at a constant temperature of 2500 °C. The igniter is 

positioned in direct contact with the solid fuel 3 mm downstream from the sample leading edge, aiming to replicate 

the igniter wire woven in the cotton fabric during the experiments. Multiple devices are positioned on the sample 

surface and further in the domain to facilitate tracking of temperature, heat flux, burning rate, and flow velocity.   

The physical properties and kinetic parameters for cotton are based on reported data for cellulose, which is 

representative of the primary composition of cotton. The thermo-physical property values for cotton are assumed 

constant and as follows: k=0.0552 W/mK, ρ=417 kg/m3, and cp=1380 J/kgK [26]. The solid fuel emissivity was set to 

ε = 0.9. The solid phase decomposition is modeled with a first order Arrhenius reaction. Although cotton is a charring

III. Results 

A. Pyrolysis Front and Spread Rate Calculations 
 

These numerical results are compared to experiments from Ref. [34]. The primary data collected during the 

experiments were the evolution of the pyrolysis front and burnout front positions (Fig. 3) over time for the different 

ambient pressures tested. Representative frames with the flame spreading over the cotton sample during a test are 

shown in Fig. 3 (left and center). Figure 3 (left) shows a frame with a strobe light on showing the pyrolysis front as a 

reference. Experimentally, the position of the pyrolysis front was defined as the point where the fabric is first visibly 

blackened as it propagates over the material. Figure 3 (center) presents a frame with the light off and shows the flame 

burnout front as well some cotton residue smoldering after the passing of the flame. Experimentally, the burnout front 

position was defined as the upstream edge of the flame, which coincides with where the flame begins receding. During 

each test, after ignition is achieved, the flame spreads uniformly along the surface of the sample. Usually, after the 

ignition of the sample, as the flame spreads over the fabric it consumes most of the cotton, leaving behind some 

smoldering cotton residue (bottom of Fig. 3 (center)) that eventually burns out most of the fabric. As ambient pressure 

was reduced, ignition of the sample was more difficult, and, in occasions, part of the cotton fabric would be left 

unburnt.

B. Effect of Reduced Ambient Pressure  
 

Figure 4 shows a series of snapshots for both the simulation results and the experiments. Figure 4 (top and middle) 

row shows the heat released rate (HRR) per unit volume, which is proportional to the reaction rate, for cases with 

different kinetic models in air at different ambient pressure. Each image also includes the corresponding time stamp 

at which the snapshot was taken. As mentioned before, all simulations presented here are generated using the DNS 

mode. There are some clear differences in between the flames generated with infinitely fast chemistry and with finite 

rate chemistry. In the first case, when infinitely fast chemistry is assumed, it is expected that combustion will happen 

as soon as the flammable mixture is formed and, thus, the reaction is controlled only by the transport and mixing 

process of the fuel and oxidizer. In the second case, when a finite rate chemistry is considered, the model relies on the 

reaction rate following an Arrhenius reaction calculated from the kinetic parameters previously defined. From the 

results shown in Fig. 4 (top and middle) it is seen that chemistry is not expected to play a role at high pressures (~100 

kPa) since the flame propagation and burning process is mainly controlled by heat and mass transport, thus little 

differences between the two cases are observed. However, as pressure is reduced from 100 kPa to 30 kPa, the chemistry 

starts to play an increasingly important role showing a lower HRR for both cases considered. Furthermore, with the 

kinetic parameters considered, the finite rate chemistry model leads to blow-off after the flame gets away from the 

igniter at 50 kPa, and eventually no-propagation at 30 kPa. However, the experiments performed at 30 kPa still showed 

flame propagation, which indicates the kinetics are not well representing the case at very low pressures. 

Figure 4 (bottom) shows snapshots of experiments performed at similar conditions of ambient pressure. Flames 

spread visibly slower as pressure is reduced. Interestingly, it is seen that experimentally as the ambient pressure is 

reduced the flame characteristics are also affected, becoming less turbulent and with its intensity reduced. As pressure 

is reduced, visible flame height is also affected, becoming shorter at lower pressures. 

Further analysis of the model results shown in Fig. 4 suggests that as pressure decreases, the HRR level (related 

to flame intensity) throughout the flame is also decreased. This is true for both fast and finite rate chemistry, although 

it is much clearer in the finite rate chemistry case (middle row). On the other hand, the distance from the flame to the 

solid surface does not seem visibly affected by the decrease in ambient pressure, regardless of the kinetic model, if far 

away from extinction conditions. These results seem to indicate that, in the FDS simulations, the reduction in flame 

spread rate with decreasing ambient pressure can be mainly attributed to a decrease in HRR. The decrease in HRR

 The pyrolysis front data from Figure 5 (a) is used to determine the flame spread rates. Figure 6 shows average 

flame spread rate obtained as a function of ambient pressure for both the experiments and the FDS simulations. 

Because of the size of the samples, and the accelerative characteristics of the flame, an averaged spread rate over the 

last 50% of the sample is presented so that the data are closer to the steady state value and transient effects are 

minimized, as explained in section A. Experimental and numerical flame spread rates decrease with ambient pressure, 

although only the results obtained with DNS and infinitely fast kinetics seem to agree well in terms of order of 

magnitude. From the results, it is seen that the simulations performed using finite rate chemistry underpredict the 

experimental spread rates at the higher pressures, while not even allowing flame spread at the lower pressure 

environments. This underprediction of the experimental data is likely associated with the selection of the chemical 

parameters chosen for the model and the differences that could exist between cellulose and the cotton fabric. It should 

be noted that under this condition only partial burning of the sample near the igniter region was observed in the FDS 

simulations done at 50 kPa, and the flame would eventually blow off about 11 mm away from the igniter, thus no 

spread rate was determined for that condition due to the very short propagation length.  
Looking to decrease the effect of ambient pressure on the simplified global one step Arrhenius reaction rate, in 

Ref. [37] different approaches were taken, looking into changes of the pre-exponential factor and the activation energy 

values as well as changes in the reaction order. As part of the sensitivity analysis performed in Ref. [37] the authors 

found that a decrease in the activation energy (Egas) of about 4% increased the flame spread rate in about 0.8%, while 

an increase in the pre-exponential factor (Agas) of about 50% only increased the flame spread rate in less than 1.4% 

affecting in both cases very little the flame spread rate observed. Additional results presented also in Ref. [37] showed 

that decreasing the order from 2 to 1 described the experimental results very well. This indicates that different 

pathways are able to reach the same experimental behavior: either using infinitely fast chemistry or with an appropriate 

(reduced) reaction order. The implications are varied. If conditions are far from extinction, it can be assumed that 

kinetic effects are non-dominant and can be neglected in a first order analysis. As hypothesized, transport processes 

determine the spread rates. Thus, the rest of the study will focus on infinitely fast chemistry for simplicity. This 

assumption will not hold near extinction conditions.  

As the flame spreads over the solid surface there are multiple complex mechanisms that involve the interaction 

between the solid phase (heat transfer, thermal decomposition, gasification) and the gas phase (transport, mixing, 

chemical kinetics) [1–3]. Depending of the conditions, some of these mechanisms can be more or less relevant for the 

general process. The following results are only presented for simulations using DNS with infinitely fast gas phase 

kinetics, because they show good agreement with the experimental results.

C. Effect of reduced ambient gravity 
 

The results of Fig. 7 agree with the hypothesis that suggest that reduced pressure can be used to replicate the flame 

spread behavior observed at reduced gravity level. Continuing with this analysis, different gravity levels are also 

simulated using FDS to explore the similarities and differences between reduced pressure and gravity. Fig. 9 shows 

the average flame spread rate obtained from the FDS simulations at normal ambient pressure, i.e. 100 kPa, for different 

gravity levels. As a comparison, the experimental data obtained in 1g at different ambient pressures and that of Saffire 

in microgravity are also included. It is seen that as the gravity level is reduced the flame spread rate follows a linear 

trend, similar to what was observed when reducing ambient pressure (experimentally and through the simulations). It 

is also seen that flame spread rates obtained at different gravity levels could be comparable to those obtained at 

different ambient pressures. In such a case, flame spread rates under lunar gravity (0.17g) could be replicated by a 

reduction in ambient pressure to 46 kPa, and flame spread rate in a martian gravity (0.38g) would be similar to that 

encountered at 54 kPa.

IV. Conclusion 

The concurrent flame spread rate over a thin cotton fabric has been studied under reduced ambient pressure and 

gravity levels using numerical simulations performed with FDS to better understand the effect of this environmental 

variable on the flame spread process.

The results show that as pressure is reduced, the flame spread rate over the cotton fabric is also reduced, as observed 

with other fuels. FDS simulations show that at higher ambient pressures the chemistry does not play a significant role 

and the process is mainly controlled by convective heat transfer mechanisms. The simulation results suggest that 

reducing the pressure decreases the HRR from the flame to the solid surface due to a reduction in convective heat flux 

at low pressures. However, the flame stand-off distance seems relatively insensitive to ambient pressure. As ambient 

pressure is further reduced, the resolution of the chemistry model can become a key factor in the definition of the 

spread rate and controlling extinction. Decoupling chemical effects from heat transfer effects shows that the chemical 

kinetic parameters selected are particularly important at lower ambient pressure environments where, if a detailed 

finite rate model is used, no propagation is observed.  

Furthermore, the analysis of the FDS simulation results suggest that as gravity levels are reduced, flame spread 

rates also decrease but the explanation behind this observation seems different. As gravity levels are lowered, the 

convective heat flux on the sample surface is reduced (as with lower pressure). This reduction in convective heat flux 

is accompanied by a visible enlargement of the boundary layer thickness, leading to a much lower flame spread rate 

as a result of the superposition of both effects. This agrees with previous analytical studies, that predicted a reduced 

buoyant flow would have these effects but highlights the differences between reducing gravity or ambient pressure.
