<p>MULTISCALE MODELING OF THE UPTAKE OF HYDROGEN CHLORIDE ON 
ANODIZED ALUMINUM IN RELEVANCE TO SPACECRAFT FIRE SAFETY 
</p>
<p> 
 
</p>
<p>Justin Niehaus1,2, Sandip Mazumder2 
 
</p>
<p>1NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, Ohio, 44135, USA 
2Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, The Ohio State University, Columbus, OH 
</p>
<p>43210, USA 
 
</p>

<p>ABSTRACT 
Fire safety is a critical component of space missions. An 
</p>
<p>electrical fire within the spacecraft often produces gaseous 
</p>
<p>hydrogen chloride (HCl) from the smoldering of wire insulation. 
</p>
<p>These gases tend to stick to the interior materials of the 
</p>
<p>spacecraft, which is often comprised of treated aluminum. 
</p>
<p>Determining the rate of uptake for HCl by aluminum helps the 
</p>
<p>safe design of the spacecraft to reduce catastrophic mission risk. 
</p>
<p>Our previous studies showed that a one-step global reaction 
</p>
<p>model for adsorption of HCl on aluminum is incapable of 
</p>
<p>predicting the experimentally measured HCl uptake of anodized 
</p>
<p>aluminum, compared to other treatments with thinner oxide 
</p>
<p>layers. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy revealed that 
</p>
<p>aluminum chloride is found deep in the oxide layer of the 
</p>
<p>anodized aluminum, implying that the transport of HCl within 
</p>
<p>the oxide layer covering the aluminum surface must be 
</p>
<p>considered. This work uses a multiscale modeling approach to 
</p>
<p>predict the uptake of HCl. A pore model is developed wherein the 
</p>
<p>HCl reacts with the aluminum oxide pore walls to create an 
</p>
<p>aluminum chloride layer. The HCl then diffuses through that 
</p>
<p>layer for further reaction, until the diffusion resistance grows too 
</p>
<p>large, and the surface is said to be saturated. The pore model is 
</p>
<p>then coupled with the reactor-scale model to predict overall HCl 
</p>
<p>uptake. Results show that this new multiscale approach predicts 
</p>
<p>the uptake of HCl far more accurately than previously used 
</p>
<p>single-scale models. 
</p>
<p>Keywords: Spacecraft Fire Safety, Multiscale Modeling, 
</p>

<p>INTRODUCTION 
 
</p>
<p>As missions in space become longer and more complex, fire 
</p>
<p>safety becomes a critical issue. Most fire testing that has been 
</p>
<p>used in spacecraft design takes place in 1-g or at small-scale [1-
</p>
<p>3]. There are experiments underway to address gaps in 
</p>
<p>knowledge of smoke and flame spread in a large-scale 
</p>
<p>microgravity environment, as well as provide the opportunity to 
</p>
<p>test novel gas species sensor technology and understand the 
</p>
<p>effect the fire has on the vehicle [4-7]. One of these knowledge 
</p>
<p>gaps is the fate of acid gas after a fire. 
</p>
<p> Risk assessments cite an electrical fire as a common 
</p>
<p>spacecraft scenario [8]. Combustion products from the 
</p>
<p>smoldering of wire insulation include acid gases such as </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 2  
</p>
<p>hydrogen chloride (HCl), hydrogen fluoride (HF) and hydrogen 
</p>
<p>cyanide (HCN). These gases pose a threat to crew health and 
</p>
<p>safety, as well as provide an opportunity for additional fire 
</p>
<p>detection methods. It is important to study the interaction of 
</p>
<p>these gases with surfaces to determine their lifetime within the 
</p>
<p>spacecraft environment for potential fire detection and post-fire 
</p>
<p>cleanup applications, as well as general crew safety. These gases 
</p>
<p>adsorb on surfaces and participate in other reactions, unlike more 
</p>
<p>common combustion products such as water and carbon dioxide. 
</p>
<p>Studies in terrestrial fires show that HCl from pyrolyzed 
</p>
<p>poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC) reached a peak in the atmosphere and 
</p>
<p>then decreased rapidly in rooms made of Poly(methyl 
</p>
<p>methacrylate) (PMMA), painted gypsum, ceiling tile and cement 
</p>
<p>block [9,10].  
</p>
<p>Aluminum is abundant inside a spacecraft and is often 
</p>
<p>treated to enhance its natural oxide layer, known as alumina [11, 
</p>
<p>12]. Chromate conversion coating (CCC) creates a thin enough 
</p>
<p>barrier to allow electricity to still be conducted through the 
</p>
<p>metal, while anodization creates a much thicker and protective 
</p>
<p>alumina layer that cannot conduct electricity. Both these 
</p>
<p>treatments are commonly used in spacecrafts. 
</p>
<p>Previous work has studied the interaction of HCl with 
</p>
<p>treated and untreated aluminum [13,14]. A reactor was built to 
</p>
<p>flow HCl over a cylindrical sample, measuring the inlet and 
</p>
<p>outlet concentration of HCl to determine the rate of uptake. A 
</p>
<p>numerical model of the reactor was developed where a one-step 
</p>
<p>global reaction that accounted for active surface sites was used 
</p>
<p>for modeling HCl uptake. The model reaction rate constants were 
</p>
<p>calibrated to the untreated and CCC aluminum experimental 
</p>
<p>results. When used for anodized aluminum, though, the same 
</p>
<p>model did not match as well with experimental data. The 
</p>
<p>untreated aluminum and the CCC aluminum were inspected 
</p>
<p>using X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) and found to 
</p>
<p>have oxide layer thicknesses of 50 and 200 nm respectively, 
</p>
<p>while the anodized aluminum had an oxide layer thickness of 
</p>
<p>5,500 nm. With an order of magnitude thicker oxide layer, the 
</p>
<p>mass transport resistance of the oxide layer becomes critical and 
</p>
<p>the effect of diffusion through the pores of the oxide layer must 
</p>
<p>be accounted for.  
</p>
<p>On account of the huge disparity in length scales, it is not 
</p>
<p>feasible to model the diffusion through the pores of the oxide 
</p>
<p>layer within the reactor-scale model. In other words, the same 
</p>
<p>mesh that resolves the inside of the reactor cannot be also used 
</p>
<p>to resolve the pores within the oxide layer covering the 
</p>
<p>aluminum surface. It is with this difficulty in mind, a separate 
</p>
<p>pore-scale model was first developed and used to compute the 
</p>
<p>growth of the aluminum chloride layer and its effect on HCl 
</p>
<p>uptake [15]. This subgrid-scale model assumes the pores to be 
</p>
<p>cylindrical and the reaction of HCl with aluminum oxide pore 
</p>
<p>walls to create aluminum chloride is considered. The HCl then 
</p>
<p>must diffuse through the thickening aluminum chloride layer to 
</p>
<p>react. The reaction stops when the diffusion resistance becomes 
</p>
<p>too large for more HCl to make it to the pore wall. The output of 
</p>
<p>this pore model was ultimately used in the reactor-scale model 
</p>
<p>to result in what is referred to here as a multiscale model. This is 
</p>
<p>accomplished by increasing the diffusion resistance realized in 
</p>
<p>the pore model. Using the time dependent decrease in diffusion 
</p>
<p>coefficient, an expression for the diffusion coefficient as a 
</p>
<p>function of HCl wall concentration is further developed to make 
</p>
<p>the expression more general and applicable to other simulation 
</p>
<p>parameters. The overall idea is depicted in Figure 1.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 1: Multiscale model schematic. A 3D rendering of the 
</p>
<p>reactor model is given by (A), where HCl only reacts with 
</p>
<p>active surface sites. When the sites are filled, the sample is 
</p>
<p>saturated, and the reaction stops. The pore model allows HCl to 
</p>
<p>diffuse into the porous oxide layer (B) and react with the 
</p>
<p>aluminum oxide walls to create an aluminum chloride product 
</p>
<p>layer (C). 
</p>

<p>Pore Model 
 
To account for the mass transport and reaction (formation of 
</p>
<p>aluminum chloride) within the oxide layer, a pore-scale model 
</p>
<p>was developed for a single pore in the oxide layer. A 2D 
</p>
<p>axisymmetric model of a single cylindrical pore was used for the 
</p>
<p>diffusion of HCl into the pore, with the top boundary condition 
</p>
<p>being of constant concentration and a boundary condition set at 
</p>
<p>the pore walls representing the reaction of HCl with aluminum 
</p>
<p>oxide. In this model, the reaction at the pore wall creates a solid 
</p>
<p>aluminum chloride layer that the HCl must diffuse through to 
</p>
<p>further react (see bottom right subfigure of Fig. 1). Once the 
</p>
<p>layer grows too thick, the sample is said to be saturated and the 
</p>
<p>reaction slows to a stop. HCl transport through the pore is 
</p>
<p>described by the 2D transient diffusion equation given by: 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>                  (5) 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The concentration of HCl in mol/m3 is denoted by c while the 
</p>
<p>diffusion coefficient in m2/s is denoted by D. The concentration 
</p>
<p>of HCl at every control volume (cell) center is determined by 
</p>
<p>discretizing Eq. (5) using the finite volume method [18]. Time 
</p>
<p>advancement is implemented using the implicit backward Euler 
</p>
<p>method. When HCl reaches the pore wall, it creates a solid 
</p>
<p>aluminum chloride and deposits on the pore wall at the rate given 
</p>
<p>by: 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>     (6) 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>
<p>are given by kr, cw, and Apo, respectively. The molar mass of the 
</p>
<p>product (aluminum chloride) is represented by M and dt is the 
</p>
<p>time step. In a particular cell, the diffusion coefficient can be 
</p>
<p>represented by the open pore diffusion coefficient (Dop), the 
</p>
<p>product layer diffusion coefficient (Dpl) or an effective diffusion 
</p>
<p>coefficient (Deff) somewhere in between Dop and Dpl for when the 
</p>
<p>product layer does not completely fill the cell during a particular 
</p>
<p>time step. A diffusion resistance is used to determine Deff through 
</p>
<p>the partially filled cell as given by: 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>  (7) 
</p>
<p>

<p>The volume fraction taken up by the produce layer and the open 
</p>
<p>pore are given by εpl and εop, respectively.

<p>      The further development of the reactor model to incorporate 
</p>
<p>pore-scale phenomenon will allow for more accurate predictions 
</p>
<p>at reduced computational costs. This is essential as spacecraft 
</p>
<p>fire simulations will be in the length scale of meters, while the 
</p>
<p>physics that govern acid gas adsorption will be micro- and nano-
</p>
<p>scale in length. More accurate predictions of the uptake of acid 
</p>
<p>gas in a fire scenario will help engineers design better smoke 
</p>
<p>detectors, and post-fire cleanup practices. Knowing how much 
</p>
<p>HCl remains in the atmosphere and how much remains in the 
</p>
<p>surfaces will aid in the development of procedures for crew 
</p>
<p>health and safety in terms of when it is optimal to return to a fire 
</p>
<p>damaged section of the spacecraft.

<p>CONCLUSION 
 
</p>
<p>Two models were utilized and eventually combined to predict 
</p>
<p>the uptake of HCl onto anodized aluminum. A single-scale 
</p>
<p>reactor model with a 1-step global reaction was able to predict 
</p>
<p>uptake of treated aluminum with thin oxide layers better than 
</p>
<p>with thick oxide layers. A multiscale model was developed to 
</p>
<p>address aluminum samples with thick oxide layers. In this model 
</p>
<p>information derived from a pore-scale model was integrated with 
</p>
<p>the reactor-scale model. The pore-scale model was utilized to 
</p>
<p>predict the uptake of HCl by the thick oxide layer of the anodized 
</p>
<p>aluminum. In the pore-scale model, the saturation of the sample 
</p>
<p>was due to the increased aluminum chloride layer build up at the 
</p>
<p>pore walls which increased the diffusion resistance of the HCl to 
</p>
<p>the pore wall, slowing the reaction to a stop. An expression of 
</p>
<p>this increased diffusion resistance was used in the reactor model 
</p>
<p>of the anodized aluminum to increase the prediction accuracy. 
</p>
<p>This expression took the form of a decreased diffusion 
</p>
<p>coefficient as a function of time in the cells adjacent to the 
</p>
<p>reacting surface. The expression was then modified to be a 
</p>
<p>function of integrated HCl wall concentration so that it could be 
</p>
<p>used in other reactor models and spacecraft simulations.  
</p>
