<p>The hazardous product release results from seven long-duration low-gravity fire tests are 
presented. These tests were conducted in low-earth orbit aboard a Cygnus spacecraft where 
the atmosphere pressure and oxygen concentration were varied to examine the effect of 
exploration atmosphere conditions. All of the samples were 40 cm wide. Four samples were 
PMMA and were 18 cm long. Two of these samples burned on both sides and the other two 
sample were attached to a plate that prevented fire on one side. The other three samples were 
cotton or a composite cotton/fiberglass blend that were 50 cm long. The cabin concentration 
of CO and CO2 were measured continuously in addition to the aerosol concentration. The 
aerosol concentration was determined using a light scattering device. The heat release from

<p>I. Introduction 
EVELOPMENT of systems and processes to ensure spacecraft fire safety requires an understanding of the 
behavior of fires in low-gravity and their impact on the habitability of the spacecraft. Prior to the SAFFIRE 
</p>
<p>(Spacecraft Fire Safety Demonstration Project) experiment series, no tests of practical scale fires had been conducted 
in low gravity. The SAFFIRE series began in 2017 with SAFFIRE I through III occurring between 2017 and 2019 [1] 
[2]. This series focused primarily on observation of flame spread and growth for large samples of thin materials in 
flights I and III and a series of smaller samples for flight II [3]. The impact on the spacecraft was deliberately 
minimized. Subsequently, flights IV-VI studied large, thick, samples that were allowed to burn for longer durations, 
releasing heat and smoke into the spacecraft cabin. To evaluate the impact of the smoke and heat, a remote diagnostics 
and filtration system, referred to as the Far Field Diagnostics (FFD) was positioned at the other end of the spacecraft 
and 6 sensors were distributed across the open volume. Through these tests, it was possible to observe the growth rate 
of fires on large thick samples and to assess the impact of these fires on the spacecraft. The tests revealed important 
features of flame spread and growth on both thick [4] [5] and thin [6] materials. These results included delayed 
extinguishment and reignition of both thick and thin materials [6]; further evidence of the effect of the duct size on 
spread rate of thin materials; [6] the strong effect of surface structures (ribs) when aligned with the flow direction; [4] 
and concurrent flame spread on thick materials is suppressed at low flow rates due to the “oxygen shadow.” [6] 
</p>
<p>Understanding the impact of the fire on the spacecraft habitability was a major objective of SAFFIRE IV-VI. The 
aerosol and gaseous species levels were measured for SAFFIRE IV-V [7] and their transport in the spacecraft was 
effectively modeled [8]. However, the fire heat release rate (HRR) was not successfully determined for SAFFIRE IV 
and V, so it was difficult to correlate the products with the fire size. For SAFFIRE VI, the HRR was successfully 
measured and preliminary analysis showed that, like terrestrial fires, the smoke products can be more hazardous than 
the heat produced by the fire [9]. This paper extends earlier analysis by considering the time to alarm for a smoke 
detector and the state of the spacecraft habitability when the alarm is triggered. The primary hazardous products from 
fires are heat, smoke, HCN, CO and CO2. NASA requirements limit CO exposure in the Spacecraft Maximum 
Allowable Concentrations (SMAC) document [10]. For CO the 1-hour SMAC is 425 ppm, and the 24-hour SMAC is 
100 ppm. For CO2, which is a metabolic product that is prevalent in crewed spacecraft, NASA-STD-3001 [11] 
establishes the  limit based on the partial pressure to no more than 3 mm Hg for a 1-hour average. Planning fire 
response depends on understanding which are the most important hazards. This paper seeks to address that question 
by examining the CO and CO2 concentration changes caused by the fire events compared to NASA exposure standards 
at the time of  alarm. These results demonstrate the importance of the hazard from fire smoke projects compared to 
the heat and pressure rise. 
</p>

<p>On each flight, the samples were installed on a single flat frame (Fig. 3) and were ignited individually in separate 
tests. The flames were imaged by 4 video cameras position to observe the top and edge of the samples. The back side 
was not imaged. During the burn sequence, the sample surface was briefly illuminated every 2 seconds for 
approximately 0.1 s by green LEDs. This enabled determination of the length of the pyrolysis zone. The parameters 
for each test were preprogramed but each test was triggered by ground command to assure continuous telemetry and 
free drift (not thruster firings) of the spacecraft. Once all of the tests were completed, the data download process began. 
Due to bandwidth limitations, the video data was compressed and only a reduced number of uncompressed images 
were downlinked. The spacecraft was initially at the pressure and oxygen concentration of the ISS but on each flight 
the Cygnus controllers vented the spacecraft to a predetermined pressure and the SFU released oxygen to bring the 
spacecraft to a targeted level.  
</p>
<p>The FFD (Fig. 4) contained a series of gas sensors as described in more detail by Fortenberry et al. [7]. These 
included gas sensors to measure CO, CO2, and O2; an ionization sensor from a commercial smoke detector; and a TSI 
DustTrak DRX 8533 which uses light scattering to measure aerosol mass concentration. The location of the FFD was 
near the inlet of the Cygnus air circulation system so the FFD provided a good assessment of the habitability of the 
spacecraft, and it could be used to assess when a smoke detector would trigger as the air circulation system sweeps 
the air toward the intake. This is similar to the ISS where the smoke detectors are in front of air intakes [12]. 
</p>
<p>The flames were tracked from the camera images using a thresholding analysis and using the average flame 
position over the central third of the flame (neglecting the edge effects). The images were adjusted for the automatic 
gain of the camera and averaged over 2 second intervals to reduce noise. In general, the trailing edge was very bright 
(soot) while the leading edge could be dim blue. The flame length was the difference between the average positions 
of the leading and trailing edges.  
</p>

<p>III. Results and Fire Impact on Habitability 
During SAFFIRE IV-VI, there were 4 flat PMMA samples successfully burned and three thin cotton-based 
</p>
<p>samples (cotton t-shirt material and SIBAL fabric) [2]. The structured PMMA tests on SAFFIRE V are discussed in 
detail by Eigenbrod et al. [4]. The 1-sided PMMA sample for SAFFIRE IV shown in Figure 3 was not ignited due to 
excessive burning of the 2-sided sample [6]. The operational conditions for these tests along with summary results are 
presented in Table 1. The flame length of the PMMA samples versus time are shown in Figure 5. Since the ignition 
time for the one-sided samples was 120 seconds (twice the 2-sided samples) the time axis is referenced to when the 
ignitor was turned off. This adjustment was only done for this figure. The other figures reference to the experiment’s 
timeline start which is approximately 10 minutes before ignition.  
</p>
<p>Unlike 1-g upward burning of thick materials such as PMMA, the flames did not spread but remained anchored at 
the leading edge and only spread as the material regressed. The PMMA samples all showed intervals of very linear 
flame growth for periods of several minutes. The slopes were generally similar ranging from 0.22 to 0.38 mm/s. This 
is significantly faster than the observations of the SAFFIRE II, 5 cm wide sample, [5] where the flame grew at less 
than 0.1 mm/s in air. The 2-sided samples are still growing at the end of the test (around 500 seconds) while the 1-
sided samples begin to plateau around 380 s for SAFFIRE V and 420 s for SAFFIRE VI. It is unknown if the 2-sided 
samples would eventually plateau, longer experiments would be helpful. A plausible explanation for the earlier plateau 
for the 1-sided samples is the enhanced heat loss through the back side versus the zero-heat loss for the 2-sided 
samples. In the case of the SAFFIRE II samples, it is likely that heat losses at the edges of the much smaller samples 
led to the growth limitation. The SAFFIRE thin samples all showed linear flame spread [6]. In comparison, 
acceleratory flame growth is often seen in normal gravity for upward flame spread on thick fuels. [13] These results 
suggest that low-gravity flame spread, and growth can be expected to be linear or less and may reach a limiting size.  
</p>
<p>Heat loss through the back side of a material is relevant in spacecraft applications, where isolated plates burning 
on both sides are uncommon. The 1-sided configuration simulates a panel or box cover with limited mass behind it. 
In the test configuration, the back side consisted of a 0.1 mm thick mica sheet (thermal diffusivity ~0.6 mm²/s) 
followed by a 0.8 mm thick aluminum card (thermal diffusivity ~97 mm²/s). Despite the large difference in diffusivity, 
the thinness of the mica layer and the high thermal conductivity of the aluminum mean that these back-side materials 
add less than 1% to the total thermal resistance of the assembly. However, the high diffusivity of aluminum will 
distribute heat laterally across the back side once the thermal wave has penetrated through the material.  
</p>
<p>The characteristic thermal penetration time for each layer can be estimated as τ = L²/α, where L is the thickness 
and α is the thermal diffusivity. This gives approximately 220 s for the 5 mm PMMA plate (α = 0.11 mm²/s), ~0.017 
s for the 0.1 mm mica sheet, and ~0.007 s for the 0.8 mm aluminum card. The combined characteristic time for the 
full assembly is therefore dominated by the PMMA layer at approximately 220 s, meaning the back-side materials do 
not meaningfully alter the thermal penetration timescale. For times greater than ~200 s, the thermal wave can be 
expected to have penetrated the full assembly thickness. The SAFFIRE VI flame growth rate results in Table 1 confirm 
that back-side heat loss had a significant effect on the growth rate. Since testing all possible configurations is

<p> 
This linear growth rate is an important finding as it suggests that even in high oxygen conditions, low-gravity 
</p>
<p>flame spread and growth will not be acceleratory. Figure 6 shows very close agreement, in the later portion of the test, 
between the flame length and the radiometer signal for the 2-sided PMMA sample in SAFFIRE VI-3. Flame tracking 
was halted when the flow was reduced to 5 cm/s at 480 s, as the flame had weakened dramatically. The primary 
purpose of this low-flow interval was to cool the sample material and ensure extinguishment upon flow termination, 
thereby avoiding the issues seen in SAFFIRE IV-2 [6]. Rather than dropping immediately, the radiometer 
measurements continued to rise steadily for an additional 50s. This behavior can be attributed to two simultaneous 
effects: the thermal expansion of the weakened flame at reduced flow, which shortened the distance between the flame 
front and the radiometers, and the accumulation of hot combustion products within the chamber resulting from the 
lower flow rate.  
</p>
<p>Combined with the absence of spread discussed above, flame growth on thick materials in low-gravity is very 
different from normal-gravity. The growth rate trends for the 2-sided samples show a 73% increase in the spread rate 
from an oxygen concentration increase of 0.08 mol fraction despite the overall pressure decrease of 45%. For the 1-
sided samples growth rate was unchanged for an oxygen increase of 0.03 mol fraction with a pressure decrease of 
27%.

<p>examine this question, an alarm threshold of 2 mg/m3 of total particulate matter detected by the TSI DustTrak DRX 
in the FFD was used as a reference point (see Fig. 7 for an example). The time to achieve this concentration is tabulated 
in Table 1 along with the increase in CO2 and CO at that time interval. Also tabulated are the flame length and the 
HRR at detection. This detection threshold detected the fire before the SAFFIRE IV PMMA sample and SAFFIRE 
VI SIBAL sample reached hazardous CO levels however both PMMA samples from SAFFIRE VI exceeded the CO 
threshold with the 1-sided sample approaching the 1-hour SMAC. Notably the CO increase was worse for the 1-sided 
sample. The SAFFIRE VI results suggest that a lower threshold or additional detectors would be needed for this 
spacecraft configuration.

<p>IV. Conclusion 
Overall, the SAFFIRE IV-VI test series provided useful validation that flame growth rate for flat materials can be 
</p>
<p>expected to exhibit linear growth without the acceleration seen in 1-g. This fact is extremely significant when 
considering the timeline of the response to a spacecraft fire. In the case of the 1-sided PMMA samples, this growth 
reached a limiting size which is even more significant.  It is possible that the 2-sided samples were not burned long 
enough to reach a limiting size.  
</p>
<p>For this configuration spacecraft, a 2 mg/m3 detection threshold was not low enough to detect the fires before some 
cases exceeded the 24-hour SMAC for CO, however it only approached the 1-hour SMAC for one case. These results 
suggest that implementing smoke detection in this spacecraft for these design fires would require either a lower 
threshold or additional smoke detectors to reduce the transport time. Proper selection of fire detection and response 
systems depends on the ability to assess whether the smoke detector can identify a fire before the spacecraft is 
uninhabitable. An assessment of this would require analysis specific to the spacecraft volume and flow conditions 
using heat and smoke production data such as this in a model of the planned spacecraft.
