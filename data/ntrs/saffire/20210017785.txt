As part of SAFFIRE V experiments on the ISS supply spacecraft CYGNUS, flame 

propagation along different surface structures on a PMMA sample was investigated in 

opposed flow. The sample was 200 x 400 mm (length x width) with a thickness of 10 mm and 

contained ribs of different widths (1-10 mm), each 3 mm high, on both sides, arranged in the 

flow direction. The total thickness of the specimen was thus 16 mm for the ribs. For the first 
360 s after ignition with a Kanthal wire, the flow velocity was 20 cm/s in the opposed 

direction. Then the flow velocity was reduced to 5 cm/s for a duration of 300 s before it was 

switched off to terminate the experiment. The experimental pressure was 761 hPa and the 

oxygen concentration was 26.9 vol %. These conditions correspond to atmospheric 

conditions envisioned for future exploration missions. It was found that the flames ignited 
downstream propagated forward along the rib edges surprisingly fast with up to 0.88 mm/s 

(1 mm rib width). Also, it was observed that the wider the rib, the slower the propagation. 

The widest rib (10 mm) already showed two largely independent edge flames. 

Unintentionally, the experiment showed how sensitive the flames are to small perturbations 

of the flow field, as remnants of a preceding and upstream experiment disturbed the 

incoming flow stratification partially.

If a sample of PMMA is ignited under 1g conditions, then large temperature gradients in conjunction with 

buoyancy convection ensure the emergence of strong velocity gradients in both upward and downward combustion. 

This leads to a local stretching of the flow field and consequently to the formation of vortices due to shear 

instabilities. This results in the well-known flickering of a flame, which is caused by the ingression of air with 

directional components normal to the direction of the buoyant flow. Figure 1 schematically shows the scenario of an 

upwardly directed combustion. 

Due to this mixing of air not only 

from below (in Figure 1 from left), there 

is always sufficient oxidant available for 

the pyrolysis gas. This leads to a blazing 

flame and to the fact that flame length 

and pyrolysis area grow steadily and the 

pyrolysis area spreads rapidly upwards 

over the surface of the sample. The air 

mixing also means that the combustion 

of the excess pyrolyzate can take place 

almost completely, at least in the case of 

the PMMA investigated here, so that 

hardly any soot or unburned MMA are 

produced. The main gases leaving the 

reaction zone at the top (to right in Figure 1) are CO2 and water. 

This is probably the most important difference in its effect to a corresponding fire scenario under microgravity 

and forced flow. In microgravity, however, the flow field remains stratified through the reaction zone in laminar 

flow despite thermal expansion and local density gradients due to the lack of weight differences. As a result, 

atmospheric oxygen can only reach the flame from upstream. This case, which is simpler in principle, has 

surprisingly far-reaching consequences. Figure 2 shows schematically the phenomena of µ g combustion under 

forced concurrent flow conditions. 

By maintaining the stratification, only 

the flame base is sufficiently supplied 

with oxygen. The stationary flame length 

is as limited as its burning rate
4

the flame, the material is heated up to the 

pyrolysis temperature, but pyrolyzate is 

protected by the flame against the entry 

of atmospheric oxygen and carried away 

with the flow. In addition to the exhaust 

gas, a considerable amount of potentially 

combustible flue gas is thus also 

produced. Since only the flame base is 

well oxygenated, there is no possibility for the burning zone to propagate across the material surface. Instead, the 

pyrolysis front propagates to a length where the radiant power of the flame is insufficient to reach the pyrolysis 

temperature. The flame base propagates at the rate at which the material is completely consumed at the leading edge. 

For given atmospheric conditions, the propagation speed of the flame base therefore depends mainly on the 

thickness of the material. The propagation of the burning zone with the flow, which is self-evident on Earth, is 

therefore rather unlikely in microgravity. If one increases the inflow velocity, from a certain flow velocity on, the

Flame propagation against the incident flow occurs on Earth very slowly compared to its spread rate in 

concurrent flow. As this propagation happens in the flow boundary layer with decreasing velocity towards the wall it

an increase of the local burning rate, but also reduces the velocity of the flame base against the incident flow. This is 

different in microgravity: even at low flow velocities, the flame base propagates rapidly against the flow. The faster 

the incoming flow becomes, the more oxygen reaches the base and the faster the flame propagates against it. Figure

concurrent and opposed combustion are comparable

remain different. What actually propagates is the material edge and with it inevitably the flame base.

The experiment first 

starts with a 10 min phase 

during which the resistance 

heating wire is energized 

with a power of 2.5 W for 

10 min. This heats the 

ignition region of the 

sample, and facilitates the 

subsequent ignition with 

30 W for one minute. This 

takes into account the 

experience with previous 

experiments with thick 

PMMA samples that this 

minimizes the risk of 

burning through the heating 

wire during the otherwise 

longer ignition phase and 

also makes it easier to

reducing the risk of

incidentally provides a second experimental condition. 

During the experiment, the progress of combustion is recorded frontally (as in Figure 8) at a frequency of 30 fps 

and from the side at the same frequency in the visible spectral range. A green LED light is used to expose the sample 

with a frequency of 0.5 Hz for three frames (0.1 s).

The first indication that the incident flow is not perfectly stratified everywhere is provided by the moment of 

ignition. This occurs in a deflagration, which is typical for the ignition of a premixture gradually developing during 

the warm-up period and which was also observed during earlier experiments, but here it is much more violent than 

normal. The Figure 9 shows successive images of the moment of ignition. The ignition point from which the flame 

spreads across the entire width of the sample can also be seen. The rather strong deflagration is probably due to the 

fact that the slightly disturbed inflow led to a partial mixing and dilution of the pyrolysis gases. This leads to delayed 

ignition until a sufficient concentration of OH radicals has accumulated with subsequent thermal runaway, and a 

larger quantity of premixed combustible fuel-air mixture has accumulated. 

Figure 10 shows two snapshots during the steady flow of 20 cm/s (after 260 s) and during the steady flow of 

5 cm/s (after 600 s). It can be clearly seen how the flame base propagates along the ribs. The propagation speed is 

impressive, as is the precision with which propagation speed and rib width are correlated. In the following, the ribs 

are labeled 1 to 10 according to their width in mm from top to bottom in Figure 10. At first glance, it can be seen 

that either rib 7 lags a little, or ribs 8-10 lead a little. This will become clearer in the following quantitative 

evaluation. The cause for this is also suspected to be an inflow that does not remain perfectly stratified and fires up 

the flames due to the associated mixing in of additional oxidizer. It can also be seen immediately that already the 

flame front of rib 2 shows a rectangular profile, corresponding to two closely spaced propagation fronts along the 

two edges. As the rib width increases, the two fronts become more distinct and different velocities become visible. 

Nevertheless, the two edge flames influence each other because the difference in front positions along a rib remains 

limited. These differences balance out with decreasing firing rate at 5 cm/s. Here, obviously, the individual edge 

flame loses strength and the range of interaction increases accordingly. Both during 20 cm/s and during 5 cm/s, two 

flame fronts can be recognized in the case of ribs 8-10. During the progressive combustion, a total of 4 flames 

become visible on these ribs. It is assumed that the trailing flame front is the shining through of the rear-side flame, 

which progresses at slightly different velocities. Front and rear side can be distinguished by intensity and sharpness. 

In this case, the flame bases most ahead are located on the front side of the specimen. The fact that the difference is 

only visible at ribs 8-10 also speaks for slightly different flow conditions on the front and back side here.

One can notice that the flames, after they have detached from the trailing edge, have a limited length. This means 

that the shadowing effect
10

 through the flame prevents oxygen from reaching downstream regions. As described in 

the introduction, this results in a finite flame length in contrast to 1g conditions. The fact that the areas behind the 

flame are extinguished, although there must still be fuel raised above the base surface, means conversely that a 

flame cannot move in the opposite direction downstream as the flame isolates the fuel downstream against oxygen

burnout end. This shadowing effect is also evident in rib 2. After 290 s, during the 20 cm/s burning period, the flame 

of rib 1 reaches the leading edge of the specimen. Once there, it gradually spread out in width along the leading 

edge. After 567 s, i.e. already during the 5 cm/s burning period, it has spread in front of flame 2. At this point, flame 

2 is still 28 mm apart from the leading edge and extinguishes in the shadow of the flame in front of it. In Figure 10 

on the right, the flame of rib 2 has already gone out. 

Not only does rib 7 lag behind, it also shows the edge flames with the lowest luminosity throughout the 

experiment. During the flow is ramping down to 5 cm/s, the flames of rib 7 extinguish after 10 s. This is associated 

with an unfavorable incident flow, as will be shown later.

Here, the surface over which radiation is absorbed plays a role, as well as the heated volume into which the heat is 

conducted. According to Delzeit et al.
11

, the internal edge angle determines the propagation velocity. However, since 

this is the same for all ribs, the mutual amplification of the two edge flames, which decreases with increasing rib 

width, must cause the different propagation rate. Thus, the heating rate is initially determined by the surface/volume 

ratio. In the 2-D representation, the radiation absorbing surface area becomes the contour length via the cross-

section, and the rib volume becomes the cross-sectional area of the rib. If one divides the contour length (2 x rib 

height + rib width) by the rib cross-sectional area and applies this over the ribs, one obtains a potential curve, which 

is additionally entered in Figure 15 as a dashed green line and makes the relationship seem obvious. In fact, the 

wider ribs should not follow this curve exactly, because here there are no longer completely closed edge flames, but 

two edge flames and a flat-area flame in between, whose effects overlap. Thus, the wider the rib becomes, the more 

should the propagation velocity be below this trend. There is another interaction which determines the heating rate, 

and which is not taken into account in the heating rate defined above: if the rib´s elevation above the base plate is 

low, then the angle between the base plate and the rib (to be referred to as the exterior angle according to Delzeit et 

al.
11

) must have a cooling effect via the heat receiving surface area which is increased here compared to the heat 

emitting flame. In this respect, the height of the ribs, and not only their widths, also plays an influencing role. Since 

the heights were not varied here, its quantities remain open. Regardless of whether the wider ribs are two 

independent edge flames or two mutually amplifying edge flames, they must in any case always propagate faster 

than the flames of the flat area, and extrapolation of the trend lines into the flat area is not permissible. This becomes 

particularly clear when the inflow velocity is reduced by 75%. For all flames, the propagation velocity is reduced by 

a comparable percentage of about 75%, consistent with the thermal theory of de Ris
12

 for thick fuels that predicts 

flame spread rate varies linearly with the flow velocity. This can vice versa be taken as an indication that reducing or 

switching off the air circulation, if operationally possible, can be a very suitable fire-fighting measure.

If one also assumes here an influence by different inflow conditions in the area of ribs 7-10, then the values of

flame lengths of ribs 7-10 are overestimated because for all rib flames a reliable measurement of length is possible

flame. This condition was not reached for ribs 7-10 because of too short test time and/or because of swirled inflow. 

In this respect, the end of the flame remained "pinned" to the trailing edge of the specimen. For ribs 7-10 the length 

of this pinned flame at the end of each flow condition is displayed in Figure 16. Therefore no error bar is given. This 

pinned flame situation changed after the transition to 5 cm/s. During the transition, flames 8-10 began to extinguish 

for 40 s and were repeatedly reignited from the trailing edge. In particular, rib 9 exhibited this striking behavior 

which is indicated the large error bars in right part of Figure 16. After 40 s, this behavior came to rest and the flames 

of ribs 8-10 were now off the sample trailing edge. It is remarkable that all ribs now show a single closed blue 

flame, so that no individual edge flames can be detected. With a weak inflow and thus reduced flame power, the 

difference between edge behavior and surface behavior seems to balance out. 

 

In order to get a better picture of the possible effect of the inflow perturbations, intensity fluctuations of the 

flames are evaluated. The assumption is that the more turbulent and thus the more unsteady the incident flow, the 

stronger the intensity fluctuations of the flames should be. For this purpose, an evaluation range is first defined for 

each rib, which spans the length of the flame vertically and is defined horizontally for each rib in such a way that the 

luminous region is completely contained. Figure 17 shows the standard deviation of the brightness of this area from 

the mean value over the entire test period. The unit is the brightness value as deviation from the mean value and can 

take values between 0 and 255. 

Here, large deviations become apparent, especially at 20 cm/s. The deviations from the mean brightness value 

reflect the displacement and vorticity of the incident flow from Figure 12 on the right very well. Only rib 1 shows 

intensity fluctuations, which were not to be expected from Figure 12. However, the snapshot of Figure 10 already 

shows that rib 1 carries a conspicuous flame. It can be assumed that Figure 17 represents a very good image of the 

inflow conditions and that, on the basis of this, a good classification of the quantitative values can be made. The 

flames calm down at 5 cm/s and their intensity variations become very similar. Here, the flat area stands out, which 

is due to the strongly flickering flame of the back side, which shines through to the result of the front side. However, 

when looking at all the images of the recorded video, it can be assumed as certain that the position of the TC 4 

thermocouple, which lies on the evaluation line of the flat area b, shows idiosyncratic combustion processes, which 

are to be associated with the installation and bonding of the thermocouple.

IV. Conclusion 

To investigate the effect of structures on opposed flame propagation, a PMMA specimen of 10 mm basic 

thickness with vertical ribs of identical height above the basis (3 mm) and different widths (1 -10 mm) was ignited at 

the downstream end during SAFFIRE V. 

It could be shown that flames propagate very fast along exposed edges in opposed flow. This propagation 

depends on the heating rate and is therefore faster the closer the edges are to each other. The propagation velocity 

follows an inverse power relationship, which is based on the heating rate determined by the surface/volume ratio. 

In comparison to SAFFIRE II, where two edges of a wide groove were investigated in concurrent flow, it could 

be shown that flames propagate with the flow along edges or over a surface only in exceptional cases. This is 

particularly evident from the fact that the flames in this opposed flow experiments assume a finite and largely 

stationary length and do not span a burning surface during propagation. 

In both opposed and concurrent flow, the amount of potentially combustible flue gas formed under the flame 

increases with time which correlates with the increasing heated and pyrolyzing area underneath the flame. 

The velocity of propagation is largely proportional to the inflow velocity, consistent with thermal theory of de

. 

Even very small disturbances of the stratification can clearly demonstrably favor combustion. Since any active 

firefighting measure also forces a disturbance of the stable stratification, it is obvious to alternatively consider a 

shutdown of the air circulation as the primary control measure. This conclusion is strengthened primarily by the fact 

that the experimental conditions studied here are idealized conditions of inflow with as little vorticity as possible. In 

the real scenario, where the flow is affected by corners, edges, and cross-section changes, as well as by human 

activity, flow calming must be particularly effective. One lesson to be learned from the previous SAFFIRE IV 

experiment, where a deflagration destroyed the apparatus after the cutoff flow was restarted after a short period, is 

that actively cooling the still burning material by reducing the flow rather than completely shutting it down may be a 

better way to avoid flashover effects.
