</p>
<p>The effect of pressure on radiative losses in hot, warm, 
</p>
<p>and cool microgravity spherical diffusion flames 
</p>
<p> 
Kendyl A. Waddella,*, Daniel L. Dietricha, Vedha Nayagamb 
</p>
<p> 
a NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135, USA 
b Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH 44106, USA 
</p>
<p>______________________________________________________________ 
 
Abstract 
This work investigates the effects of ambient pressure on radiative heat losses in hot, warm, and cool microgravity 
spherical diffusion flames using a numerical model and compares the results to observations from microgravity 
experiments. The experimental data was obtained during droplet combustion experiments, and the numerical 
model simulates burner-supported flames. The similarities in the gas phase between the two configurations allow 
these simulations to provide accurate insights into the experimental results. The model uses a statistical narrow-
band model that includes emission and reabsorption. The effect as pressure was varied between 0.5 and 10 atm 
on the relative radiation contributions from CO2 and H2O, the primary radiating species, was examined. As the 
pressure increased H2O contributed more to radiative loss than CO2 in both the experiments and computations. 
This was determined to be caused by increased absorption coefficients leading to increased reabsorption at higher 
pressures. Calculations performed without reabsorption did not see this effect, confirming it is due to the optically 
thick nature of the system. Reabsorption was higher for CO2 due to the prominence of the absorption band at 4.3 
µm, which became saturated at higher pressures. These results demonstrated the importance of using the optically 
thick assumption to accurately account for radiative losses in these flames, which are crucial to capture flame 
behaviours such as radiative extinction and reignitions. 
 
Keywords: Radiative heat loss; Optical thickness; Cool flames; Warm flames; Microgravity 
</p>

<p>Novelty and significance statement 1 
</p>
<p>The novelty of this study is that it provides the first 2 
</p>
<p>comprehensive analysis of the effects of pressure on 3 
</p>
<p>radiative reabsorption in hot, warm, and cool 4 
</p>
<p>microgravity diffusion flames. A statistical narrow 5 
</p>
<p>band radiation model is used to explain previously 6 
</p>
<p>unexplained experimental behaviour from 7 
</p>
<p>International Space Station droplet combustion 8 
</p>
<p>experiments, where the contribution to total radiative 9 
</p>
<p>loss shifted from CO2 dominated to H2O dominated at 10 
</p>
<p>higher pressures. The results show for the first time 11 
</p>
<p>this behaviour was caused by the saturation of CO2 12 
</p>
<p>absorption bands and could not be accounted for with 13 
</p>
<p>optically thin radiation models. This demonstrates the 14 
</p>
<p>limitations of using the optically thin assumption 15 
</p>
<p>when calculating radiative loss in combustion 16 
</p>
<p>systems, particularly at elevated pressures, which has 17 
</p>
<p>significant implications for flame stability, extinction, 18 
</p>
<p>and reignition. 19 
</p>
<p> 20 
</p>
<p>1. Introduction 21 
</p>
<p>Radiative heat transfer is of great importance in 22 
</p>
<p>many combustion systems, particularly in high 23 
</p>
<p>temperature systems such as flames and fires where it 24 
</p>
<p>can be the dominant mode of heat transfer. In 25 
</p>
<p>microgravity, where natural convection is eliminated, 26 
</p>
<p>combustion products accumulate in the flame region 27 
</p>
<p>and radiate heat while the chemical heat release from 28 
</p>
<p>the flame remains relatively constant, causing the 29 
</p>
<p>flame temperature to decrease until combustion can 30 
</p>
<p>no longer be sustained. This phenomenon, known as 31 
</p>
<p>radiative extinction, has been the subject of several 32 
</p>
<p>previous studies [1–5]. Most studies are primarily 33 
</p>
<p>concerned with the total radiative loss from the flame, 34 
</p>
<p>and less so with the individual contributions from 35 
</p>
<p>different species. The primary radiating species in 36 
</p>
<p>combustion systems are H2O, CO2, CO, soot, and fuel 37 
</p>
<p>vapor. Often CO2 and H2O are the main contributors 38 
</p>
<p>to radiative heat loss.  39 
</p>
<p>Radiative heat transfer in combustion systems has 40 
</p>
<p>been studied extensively, with particular attention 41 
</p>
<p>paid to the accuracy of different radiation property 42 
</p>
<p>models and radiative transfer equation (RTE) solution 43 
</p>
<p>methods [6–11]. The main types of radiation models, 44 
</p>
<p>in order of increasing accuracy, are grey gas, wide 45 
</p>
<p>band, narrow-band, and line-by-line models. Grey gas 46 
</p>
<p>models do not account for the change of radiative 47 
</p>
<p>properties with wavelength and tend to result in 48 
</p>
<p>unacceptable accuracy. Wide band models divide the 49 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>2 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>absorption and emission spectra into a few wide bands 1 
</p>
<p>and are computationally inexpensive but still not of 2 
</p>
<p>high enough fidelity for most combustion 3 
</p>
<p>applications. Narrow band models divide the spectral 4 
</p>
<p>domain into even smaller bands and average the 5 
</p>
<p>absorption coefficient over each band. With small 6 
</p>
<p>enough bands, this method can produce comparable 7 
</p>
<p>results to the line-by-line model while greatly 8 
</p>
<p>reducing computational time. Line-by-line models 9 
</p>
<p>consider each spectral line individually and are highly 10 
</p>
<p>accurate but often too computationally expensive. In 11 
</p>
<p>general, the statistical narrow band (SNB) method 12 
</p>
<p>strikes a good balance between accuracy and 13 
</p>
<p>computational time [7,9,12–14] for detailed 14 
</p>
<p>simulations of laboratory-scale flames.  15 
</p>
<p>When solving the RTE it is common to use one of 16 
</p>
<p>two treatments. The first is that radiators both emit 17 
</p>
<p>and absorb radiation, i.e., the system is optically thick. 18 
</p>
<p>This is the more accurate treatment but greatly 19 
</p>
<p>increases computation time. The second treatment is 20 
</p>
<p>to assume radiators only emit radiation but do not 21 
</p>
<p>absorb it, i.e., the system is optically thin. This can 22 
</p>
<p>greatly reduce computation times but is often unable 23 
</p>
<p>to reproduce experimental behavior without 24 
</p>
<p>additional correction factors [6–11].  25 
</p>
<p>There have been studies that examined the effects 26 
</p>
<p>of using the optically thin or optically thick 27 
</p>
<p>assumption, [9,11] or the effects of changing stretch 28 
</p>
<p>rate [10,12] or ambient pressure [14], which affect the 29 
</p>
<p>optical thickness [10,12,14] on radiative heat transfer. 30 
</p>
<p>Bedir et al. [9] compared different radiation models 31 
</p>
<p>for a PMMA stagnation point flame. They found that 32 
</p>
<p>using the SNB model with the optically thin 33 
</p>
<p>assumption overpredicted radiative emission by as 34 
</p>
<p>much as 57%, and similar results were obtained using 35 
</p>
<p>the Planck mean absorption coefficient. Applying a 36 
</p>
<p>factor of 0.44 to the Planck mean results provided 37 
</p>
<p>good agreement with the total emission from the 38 
</p>
<p>optically thick narrow band calculations, but there 39 
</p>
<p>were still inaccuracies in the distribution of radiation.  40 
</p>
<p>Ju et al. [11] calculated new Planck mean 41 
</p>
<p>absorption coefficients using the SNB model and 42 
</p>
<p>compared the resulting heat fluxes to those obtained 43 
</p>
<p>from the SNB method for a counterflow CH4 flame. 44 
</p>
<p>They found that the Planck mean absorption 45 
</p>
<p>coefficient overpredicted radiative heat loss due to the 46 
</p>
<p>lack of radiation reabsorption. Rhatigan et al. [12] 47 
</p>
<p>used an SNB model to calculate the radiation heat 48 
</p>
<p>transfer in a PMMA stagnation point diffusion flame 49 
</p>
<p>and compared the results to using the Planck mean 50 
</p>
<p>absorption coefficient and the optically thin SNB 51 
</p>
<p>model. They found that the Planck mean and optically 52 
</p>
<p>thin SNB methods both overpredicted emission due to 53 
</p>
<p>a lack of absorption, and that the net radiative loss 54 
</p>
<p>from the flame increased with increasing stretch rate. 55 
</p>
<p>Chu et al. [14] studied the effects of total pressure on 56 
</p>
<p>radiative heat transfer in a parallel plate geometry 57 
</p>
<p>containing CO2, H2O, and mixtures of the two. They 58 
</p>
<p>showed that radiative emission and absorption 59 
</p>
<p>increased with increasing pressure due to increasing 60 
</p>
<p>optical thickness but did not differentiate between 61 
</p>
<p>CO2 and H2O emission/absorption.  62 
</p>
<p>Bedir and T’ien [10] studied radiation in a PMMA 63 
</p>
<p>stagnation point diffusion flame at varying stretch 64 
</p>
<p>rates.  They considered the radiation emission and 65 
</p>
<p>absorption contributions for CO2, H2O, and fuel 66 
</p>
<p>vapor. As the stretch rate increased the optical 67 
</p>
<p>thickness of the system decreased due to the pressure 68 
</p>
<p>path length of the radiating species decreasing. Most 69 
</p>
<p>of the emitted radiation came from CO2, and the 70 
</p>
<p>contribution to total emitted radiation from CO2 71 
</p>
<p>increased with increasing optical thickness. As optical 72 
</p>
<p>thickness increased the absorption percentage of gas 73 
</p>
<p>phase radiation increased for both CO2 and H2O, but 74 
</p>
<p>the CO2 increase was much larger. Most of the 75 
</p>
<p>absorption was determined to be self-absorption, i.e., 76 
</p>
<p>radiation from a particular species was absorbed by 77 
</p>
<p>that same species, rather than cross absorption.  78 
</p>
<p>Both droplet [1,3,8,15,16] and burner supported 79 
</p>
<p>[2,5,17–20] configurations are commonly used to 80 
</p>
<p>study microgravity spherical diffusion flames 81 
</p>
<p>experimentally and numerically. The two 82 
</p>
<p>configurations are similar, differing primarily by the 83 
</p>
<p>boundary conditions at the surface of the droplet or 84 
</p>
<p>sphere.  The burner-supported configuration is, 85 
</p>
<p>however, computationally easier (faster) to study 86 
</p>
<p>since the mass flux and temperature at the burner 87 
</p>
<p>surface are prescribed rather than being part of the 88 
</p>
<p>solution.  Both microgravity droplet and burner-89 
</p>
<p>supported combustion experiments have a gas-phase 90 
</p>
<p>that can be considered quasi-steady outside of the 91 
</p>
<p>ignition and extinction transients. This allows the 92 
</p>
<p>burner-supported configuration to provide accurate 93 
</p>
<p>insights into the gas-phase processes [3,4,20], 94 
</p>
<p>provided the boundary conditions at the burner 95 
</p>
<p>surface match the burning rate and droplet surface 96 
</p>
<p>temperature in the experiment. 97 
</p>
<p>Warm and cool diffusion flames are relatively new 98 
</p>
<p>areas of study. Cool diffusion flames were first 99 
</p>
<p>observed during droplet experiments on the 100 
</p>
<p>International Space Station (ISS) in 2012 [21], and 101 
</p>
<p>warm diffusion flames were first observed in normal 102 
</p>
<p>gravity counterflow experiments [22] and 103 
</p>
<p>microgravity droplet experiments [23] in 2018. These 104 
</p>
<p>flames burn at lower temperatures than typical hot 105 
</p>
<p>flames and follow different chemical kinetic 106 
</p>
<p>pathways. Understanding their behavior is key to 107 
</p>
<p>developing advanced engine technologies that utilize 108 
</p>
<p>their chemistry to create engines that are more 109 
</p>
<p>efficient and less polluting. These flames can also 110 
</p>
<p>exist outside the typical flammability limits of hot 111 
</p>
<p>flames and are favored to form in microgravity, 112 
</p>
<p>making them important to both terrestrial and 113 
</p>
<p>spacecraft fire safety. Compared to hot flames, there 114 
</p>
<p>has been little research into the radiative properties of 115 
</p>
<p>these flames. Lin et al. [24] modeled thermal radiation 116 
</p>
<p>in counterflow warm and cool diffusion flames but 117 
</p>
<p>used an optically thin radiation model due to short 118 
</p>
<p>optical lengths in that configuration. They found that 119 
</p>
<p>thermal radiation did affect the flammability limits of 120 
</p>
<p>the flames. Farouk et al. [23] modeled radiation in 121 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>3 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>microgravity droplet diffusion flames using an SNB 1 
</p>
<p>model and found that total radiative losses were lower 2 
</p>
<p>during the warm and cool flame burning periods 3 
</p>
<p>compared to the hot flame but did not consider the 4 
</p>
<p>relative contributions from individual species.  5 
</p>
<p>During recent droplet experiments performed 6 
</p>
<p>aboard the ISS radiance measurements were taken 7 
</p>
<p>that captured both the total flame radiance and the 8 
</p>
<p>radiance from water vapor in the 5-7 µm range at 9 
</p>
<p>various ambient pressures [16]. A trend observed 10 
</p>
<p>during these experiments was that, during the hot 11 
</p>
<p>flame, as ambient pressure increased and as the flame 12 
</p>
<p>burned the fraction of total radiation that came from 13 
</p>
<p>water vapor increased. This trend could not be easily 14 
</p>
<p>explained, as the production rates of main radiators 15 
</p>
<p>CO2 and H2O should be relatively constant between 16 
</p>
<p>conditions, and any changes to product formation 17 
</p>
<p>should affect both radiators equally. The objective of 18 
</p>
<p>this work is to use a transient numerical model of 19 
</p>
<p>burner supported flames with an optically thick SNB 20 
</p>
<p>model to recreate and determine the cause of the 21 
</p>
<p>experimental behavior. The influence of using the 22 
</p>
<p>optically thick or optically thin assumption on the 23 
</p>
<p>accuracy of the numerical predictions is also 24 
</p>
<p>examined for the spherically symmetric diffusion 25 
</p>
<p>flames. 26 
</p>
<p> 27 
</p>
<p>2. Methods 28 
</p>
<p> 29 
</p>
<p>2.1 Experimental 30 
</p>
<p>Droplet experiments were carried out aboard the 31 
</p>
<p>ISS as part of the Cool Flames Investigation (CFI), a 32 
</p>
<p>subset of the Flame Extinguishment Experiment 33 
</p>
<p>(FLEX) set of experiments. More details on the 34 
</p>
<p>experiments can be found in [15,16,25,26]. Both free 35 
</p>
<p>floated droplets and droplets supported by a 40 µm 36 
</p>
<p>carbide fiber were examined, and initial droplet 37 
</p>
<p>diameters for the pure dodecane experiments ranged 38 
</p>
<p>from 4 to 4.5 mm. The ambient was air (0.21/0.79 39 
</p>
<p>O2/N2) for most of the tests and the ambient pressure 40 
</p>
<p>ranged from 0.5 to 5 atm.  41 
</p>
<p>The droplet and flame were monitored using color, 42 
</p>
<p>backlit, and intensified cameras. Droplet sizes were 43 
</p>
<p>measured using the backlit camera, and the estimated 44 
</p>
<p>uncertainties in droplet size are ± 50 µm [15].  45 
</p>
<p>Flame radiance was measured by two radiometers, 46 
</p>
<p>one wideband and one narrowband, both located 110 47 
</p>
<p>mm away from the droplet deployment site. The 48 
</p>
<p>wideband detector was a silicon-based thermopile 49 
</p>
<p>detector with a KRS-5 window that limited the 50 
</p>
<p>spectral response from 0.58 µm to 50 µm, used argon 51 
</p>
<p>as a back-fill gas, had an active area of 0.36 mm2, and 52 
</p>
<p>was preceded by an aperture with a diameter of 3.175 53 
</p>
<p>mm. The narrowband detector was a thin-film based 54 
</p>
<p>thermopile with a silicon long-wave-pass filter and 55 
</p>
<p>sapphire window that limited the spectral range from 56 
</p>
<p>5 µm to 7 µm, where water vapor exhibits strong 57 
</p>
<p>absorbance. It used xenon as a back-fill gas, had an 58 
</p>
<p>active area of 4.0 mm2, and was preceded by an 59 
</p>
<p>aperture with a diameter of 4.572 mm [15].   60 
</p>
<p>The radiometers were calibrated using a calibrated 61 
</p>
<p>blackbody source, and total radiance was determined 62 
</p>
<p>using this calibration and details of the radiometer 63 
</p>
<p>geometry relative to the droplet and flame. 64 
</p>
<p>Uncertainties in flame radiance are estimated to be up 65 
</p>
<p>to ± 20% [15]. More details on the radiometers and 66 
</p>
<p>their calibration, as well as the conversion from 67 
</p>
<p>irradiance to flame radiance, can be found in [15]. 68 
</p>
<p> 69 
</p>
<p>2.2 Numerical 70 
</p>
<p>Numerical simulations were performed using the 71 
</p>
<p>SphDiff program [18–20,27,28], which is a 72 
</p>
<p>modification of Sandia PREMIX and uses the 73 
</p>
<p>Chemkin-II package [29]. This numerical model has 74 
</p>
<p>been used in various studies of burner supported 75 
</p>
<p>spherical diffusion flames and has been shown to 76 
</p>
<p>accurately reproduce the experimental results [18–77 
</p>
<p>20,27,28]. A full model description can be found in 78 
</p>
<p>[20,27,28]. 79 
</p>
<p>The inner boundary was the burner surface, at a 80 
</p>
<p>radius of 2.25 mm. Mass flow rate and burner 81 
</p>
<p>temperature were prescribed, and species mass flux 82 
</p>
<p>and energy were conserved. The outer boundary was 83 
</p>
<p>at a radius of 100 cm and temperature and species 84 
</p>
<p>concentrations were prescribed there. The domain 85 
</p>
<p>was sufficiently large that there was no change in 86 
</p>
<p>species or temperature near the outer boundary.  87 
</p>
<p>An adaptive grid algorithm was used, as in [20]. 88 
</p>
<p>The domain was divided into three grid areas: an inner 89 
</p>
<p>area, a reaction area, and an outer area. The inner area 90 
</p>
<p>follows the same algorithm as [28], and the reaction 91 
</p>
<p>area has 200 grid cells with 0.001 cm spacing and was 92 
</p>
<p>centered about the peak temperature. From there, the 93 
</p>
<p>grid spacing in the outer area increased by 5% for each 94 
</p>
<p>grid cell moving outward. A total of 373 grid cells 95 
</p>
<p>were used, which satisfied grid independence. 96 
</p>
<p>The radiation model is described in detail in 97 
</p>
<p>[27,28]. The model considered radiation heat loss 98 
</p>
<p>from CO, CO2, and H2O. A detailed 99 
</p>
<p>absorption/emission statistical narrow band model 100 
</p>
<p>was used to calculate the absorption coefficient for 101 
</p>
<p>these species. For optically thin simulations, 102 
</p>
<p>absorption was neglected. Radiation properties were 103 
</p>
<p>taken from the HITRAN database [30] with 104 
</p>
<p>wavenumbers ranging from 150 to 9300 cm-1. The 105 
</p>
<p>data was divided into 366 bands, each of 25 cm-1, and 106 
</p>
<p>properties were averaged over each band considering 107 
</p>
<p>a Lorentz spectral line shape of random spacing and 108 
</p>
<p>strength. The radiative transfer equation was solved 109 
</p>
<p>using a discrete-ordinates method [27,28]. Radiation 110 
</p>
<p>from soot was not considered in these simulations, as 111 
</p>
<p>experimental observations show luminous soot does 112 
</p>
<p>not typically form in these flames after the ignition 113 
</p>
<p>transient owing to the large radiative losses reducing 114 
</p>
<p>the temperature below the sooting threshold [3,4,20]. 115 
</p>
<p>For the simulations the fuel was pure heptane, 116 
</p>
<p>owing to the lack of an available dodecane chemical 117 
</p>
<p>kinetic mechanism that was compatible with SphDiff. 118 
</p>
<p>The radiative extinction characteristics of the 119 
</p>
<p>dodecane droplets were very similar to those for pure 120 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>4 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>3. Results and Discussion 19 
</p>
<p>Figure 1a shows the simulated temporal evolution 20 
</p>
<p>of flame temperature and flame radius for the 1 atm, 21 
</p>
<p>4 atm, and 10 atm cases. These simulations were 22 
</p>
<p>performed using the SNB method with emission and 23 
</p>
<p>reabsorption. The flames ignite at temperatures above 24 
</p>
<p>2000 K, after which the peak temperature decreases 25 
</p>
<p>due to increasing radiative losses. As the flame 26 
</p>
<p>temperature decreases it is possible for the flame to 27 
</p>
<p>traverse all three combustion regimes, hot, warm, and 28 
</p>
<p>cool flames, depending on the pressure.  29 
</p>
<p>As hot flame peak temperature reaches ~1200 K, 30 
</p>
<p>the hot flame either radiatively extinguishes if the 31 
</p>
<p>pressure is less than 4 atm or transitions to a warm 32 
</p>
<p>flame if the pressure is 4 atm or higher. In the cases 33 
</p>
<p>where a warm flame is established, the transition from 34 
</p>
<p>hot flame to warm flame is not obvious when looking 35 
</p>
<p>at the temperature or radius curves. Once a warm 36 
</p>
<p>flame is established it burns in a similar manner to a 37 
</p>
<p>hot flame and radiatively extinguishes before a cool 38 
</p>
<p>flame is established. This section is organized such 39 
</p>
<p>that the combustion regimes are discussed separately, 40 
</p>
<p>starting with the hot flame, and moving to the warm 41 
</p>
<p>flame and then the cool flame.  42 
</p>
<p> 43 
</p>
<p>3.1 Hot Flame 44 
</p>
<p>Figure 1a shows that the evolution of flame 45 
</p>
<p>temperature is nearly invariant with pressure during 46 
</p>
<p>the hot flame. Peak temperature is controlled by the 47 
</p>
<p>balance between radiative loss and chemical heat 48 
</p>
<p>release, both of which decrease with increasing 49 
</p>
<p>pressure due to the increasing influence of 50 
</p>
<p>intermediate-temperature HO2 chemistry, which 51 
</p>
<p>shifts chemical pathways and has a weakening effect 52 
</p>
<p>on the hot flame [32]. Also shown in Fig. 1a is the 53 
</p>
<p>flame radius, which decreases with increasing 54 
</p>
<p>pressure. Increased pressure decreases the flow 55 
</p>
<p>velocity and increases the molar density of fuel vapor 56 
</p>
<p>and oxidizer in the system, allowing the flame to 57 
</p>
<p>establish at locations closer to the burner surface. 58 
</p>
<p>The net molar production rate of the radiating 59 
</p>
<p>products H2O, CO2, and CO are shown in Fig. 1b. 60 
</p>
<p>During the hot flame H2O is produced at higher rates 61 
</p>
<p>than CO2, consistent with the stoichiometry, and the 62 
</p>
<p>production of both is relatively steady with time and 63 
</p>
<p>decreases with increasing pressure, due to the rise 64 
</p>
<p>pressure-dependent intermediate-temperature HO2 65 
</p>
<p>chemistry [32]. There is little to no CO production 66 
</p>
<p>during the hot flame.  67 
</p>
<p>The simulated temporal evolution of H2O, CO2, 68 
</p>
<p>and CO radiance, integrated across the entire 69 
</p>
<p>computational domain at each timestep, for the 1 atm, 70 
</p>
<p>4 atm, and 10 atm cases is shown in Fig. 2. Figure 2a 71 
</p>
<p>shows the raw computed values; in Fig. 2b they are 72 
</p>
<p>normalized by the flame diameter cubed at each 73 
</p>
<p>timestep to account for size effects.  74

</p>
<p>H2O and CO2. Water vapor radiance increases with 76 
</p>
<p>increasing time at all pressures due to flame growth, 77 
</p>
<p>when this is accounted for H2O radiance decreases as 78 
</p>
<p>time increases. In Fig. 2a CO2 radiance is relatively 79 
</p>
<p>constant with time following the initial ignition 80 
</p>
<p>transient. When flame size is accounted for CO2 81 
</p>
<p>radiance decreases with increasing time, and the 82 
</p>
<p>degree of this decrease is higher than that for H2O.  83 
</p>
<p>Water vapor radiance increases with increasing 84 
</p>
<p>pressure in the hot flame, while CO2 radiance 85 
</p>
<p>decreases with increasing pressure. When flame size 86 
</p>
<p>is taken into consideration that radiance from both 87 
</p>
<p>species increases with increasing pressure, though 88

<p>H2O increases to a larger extent. Since the changes in 1 
</p>
<p>production rate with pressure affect both species in the 2 
</p>
<p>same direction, the only explanation for a difference 3 
</p>
<p>in their behavior is a difference in the radiative 4 
</p>
<p>properties for each species. 5 
</p>
<p>Both as the flame burns and as pressure increases, 6 
</p>
<p>the absorption coefficients of H2O and CO2 increase 7 
</p>
<p>due to increasing molar concentrations. Increased 8 
</p>
<p>absorption coefficients lead to higher emission and 9 
</p>
<p>reabsorption of radiation. With the optically thin 10 
</p>
<p>assumption there is no reabsorption of radiation, and 11 
</p>
<p>comparing optically thin calculations to optically 12 
</p>
<p>thick calculations isolates the reabsorption effects. 13 
</p>
<p>The radiation calculations were re-performed using 14 
</p>
<p>the same temperature and species profiles that were 15 
</p>
<p>obtained with optically thick simulations, but 16 
</p>
<p>neglecting the reabsorption term from the RTE. The 17 
</p>
<p>reabsorption fraction is then calculated as 1-18 
</p>
<p>Rthick/Rthin, where R is the total radiance.  The resulting 19 
</p>
<p>reabsorption fraction (i.e., the fraction of emitted 20 
</p>
<p>radiation that was reabsorbed) is shown in Fig. 3.  21 
</p>
<p>A low reabsorption fraction indicates that most of 22 
</p>
<p>the emitted radiation escapes the system, and there is 23 
</p>
<p>little reabsorption, while a high reabsorption fraction 24 
</p>
<p>indicates most of the emitted radiation is reabsorbed 25 
</p>
<p>before it leaves the system. The reabsorption fraction 26 
</p>
<p>shows that CO2 reabsorption is significantly higher 27 
</p>
<p>than H2O reabsorption during the hot flame at all 28 
</p>
<p>pressures, and both values increase with increasing 29 
</p>
<p>pressure. This explains the trends seen in Fig. 2. 30 
</p>
<p>Radiance from CO2 is relatively constant with time 31 
</p>
<p>because early in the flame lifetime a high percentage 32 
</p>
<p>of the emitted radiation is reabsorbed. Meanwhile 33 
</p>
<p>H2O reabsorption levels are low, and as such H2O 34 
</p>
<p>radiance can continually increase with time. The same 35 
</p>
<p>is true as pressure increases, CO2 reabsorption is so 36 
</p>
<p>high that its radiance decreases with increasing 37 
</p>
<p>pressure, while H2O reabsorption is low enough that 38 
</p>
<p>its radiance can increase as pressure increases.  39 
</p>
<p>The radiance values shown in Fig. 2 for each 40 
</p>
<p>species were obtained by integrating the radiance 41 
</p>
<p>from the surface to the outer boundary, which is the 42 
</p>
<p>equivalent of the total radiative loss from the system. 43 
</p>
<p>If the integration is instead performed from the 44 
</p>
<p>surface to some intermediate radius, the result is the 45 
</p>
<p>radiative loss from the system up to that radius, which 46 
</p>
<p>is the equivalent of placing a radiometer at that point. 47 
</p>
<p>Figure 4 shows this integration for the 1 atm and 4 atm 48 
</p>
<p>cases when the peak temperature is 1300 K, 49 
</p>
<p>considering both optically thick and optically thin 50 
</p>
<p>calculations. The distribution of temperature is also 51 
</p>
<p>shown. 52 
</p>
<p>Considering CO2 in the optically thick cases, 53 
</p>
<p>moving outwards from the burner surface, there is a 54 
</p>
<p>region of net reabsorption near the burner surface, 55 
</p>
<p>after which radiance increases sharply in the high 56 
</p>
<p>temperature region near the flame. The radiance then 57 
</p>
<p>peaks and begins to decrease due to reabsorption by 58 
</p>
<p>the large concentration of accumulated products at 59 
</p>
<p>low temperature in this region and finally plateaus as 60 
</p>
<p>the temperature and species concentrations decrease. 61 
</p>
<p>For H2O the distribution is similar, but the effect of 62 
</p>
<p>reabsorption is less pronounced. These trends are the 63 
</p>
<p>same between the 1 atm and 4 atm cases, with the 4 64 
</p>
<p>atm case having a more compressed profile.  65 
</p>
<p>When reabsorption is neglected, radiance from 66 
</p>
<p>both H2O and CO2 increases continually throughout 67 
</p>
<p>the flame region until there are no more radiating 68 
</p>
<p>products. There is a much more significant difference 69 
</p>
<p>between the optically thick and optically thin cases for 70 
</p>
<p>CO2 than H2O, owing to higher levels of reabsorption.  71

<p>The differences in reabsorption are due to the 1 
</p>
<p>spectral properties of each species. Figure 5 shows the 2 
</p>
<p>absorption cross sections of H2O and CO2 at 750 K 3 
</p>
<p>and 1500 K, obtained from the HITRAN database 4 
</p>
<p>[30]. These values are multiplied by Avogadro’s 5 
</p>
<p>number and the molar concentration to obtain 6 
</p>
<p>absorption coefficient. The H2O absorption spectrum 7 
</p>
<p>is broad, while the CO2 spectrum has strong peaks 8 
</p>
<p>centered at 4.3 µm and 15 µm. When temperature 9 
</p>
<p>increases the shape of the H2O spectrum changes 10 
</p>
<p>slightly, with its magnitude decreasing at some 11 
</p>
<p>wavelengths and increasing at others. The CO2 12 
</p>
<p>spectrum keeps the same general shape as temperature 13 
</p>
<p>increases, and the magnitude of its peaks decreases. 14 
</p>
<p>The 4.3 µm band has the highest magnitude and is the 15 
</p>
<p>key to understanding the difference in reabsorption 16 
</p>
<p>behaviors between the two molecules.  17 
</p>
<p>Increasing pressure while holding other conditions 18 
</p>
<p>constant increases the molecular density of the 19 
</p>
<p>system, which increases absorption coefficient. A 20 
</p>
<p>high absorption coefficient indicates that radiation is 21 
</p>
<p>likely to be reabsorbed before it escapes the region 22 
</p>
<p>where it is emitted, with total radiative intensity 23 
</p>
<p>limited by the Planck blackbody function. When the 24 
</p>
<p>absorption coefficient is initially low, the intensity is 25 
</p>
<p>far away from this limit and can increase towards it as 26 
</p>
<p>absorption coefficient increases. When the absorption 27 
</p>
<p>coefficient is initially high, as it is in the 4.3 µm band 28 
</p>
<p>for CO2, total intensity in that band is already near the 29 
</p>
<p>limit and any new emission is almost immediately 30 
</p>
<p>reabsorbed.  31 
</p>
<p>This can be seen in Figure 6, where the radiance 32 
</p>
<p>distribution across the domain has been broken down 33 
</p>
<p>according to wavelength for the 1 atm case (with and 34 
</p>
<p>without reabsorption) and the 4 atm case (with 35 
</p>
<p>reabsorption) when the peak temperature is 1300 K. 36 
</p>
<p>Neglecting reabsorption in the 1 atm case increases 37 
</p>
<p>radiance by a fair amount across the H2O spectrum 38 
</p>
<p>and in the 15 µm band for CO2. In the 4.3 µm band 39 
</p>
<p>the amount of reabsorption is much higher, 80% of the 40 
</p>
<p>CO2 radiance in that band is reabsorbed. There is a 41 
</p>
<p>moderate increase in the radiance values in the 4 atm 42 
</p>
<p>case compared to the 1 atm case across the H2O 43 
</p>
<p>spectrum and in the 15 µm band for CO2, but in the 44 
</p>
<p>4.3 µm band the radiance mostly decreased as 45 
</p>
<p>pressure increased. 46 
</p>
<p>Figure 7 summarizes the changes in H2O and CO2 47 
</p>
<p>radiance during the hot flame as pressure changes 48 
</p>
<p>from 0.5 to 10 atm for cases with and without 49 
</p>
<p>reabsorption. Figure 7a shows the H2O and CO2 50 
</p>
<p>radiance when the flame temperature is 1300 K at 51 
</p>
<p>each pressure, normalized by the flame diameter 52 
</p>
<p>cubed to remove flame size effects. Water vapor 53 
</p>
<p>radiance increases with increasing pressure both with 54 
</p>
<p>and without reabsorption, and the increase for cases 55 
</p>
<p>without reabsorption is more significant. The same is 56 
</p>
<p>true for CO2 to an even larger extent, optically thick 57 
</p>
<p>CO2 radiance increases only slightly with increasing 58 
</p>
<p>pressure, and optically thin radiance is an order of 59 
</p>
<p>magnitude higher at higher pressures. Such a high 60 
</p>
<p>amount of reabsorption is due to saturation of the 4.3 61 
</p>
<p>µm CO2 band, keeping CO2 radiance nearly level as 62 
</p>
<p>pressure increases. 63 
</p>
<p>Figure 7b shows how the fraction of total radiance 64 
</p>
<p>that comes from CO2 and H2O (the radiance ratio) 65 
</p>
<p>changes as pressure increases for cases both with and 66 
</p>
<p>without radiation reabsorption. In cases without 67 
</p>
<p>reabsorption the radiance is consistently around 0.8 68 
</p>
<p>for CO2 and 0.2 for H2O, as the radiance for both 69 
</p>
<p>increases linearly with pressure. However, in cases 70 
</p>
<p>with reabsorption CO2 radiance dominates at lower 71 
</p>
<p>pressures, and as pressure increases the CO2 radiance 72 
</p>
<p>ratio decreases and more of the radiance comes from 73 
</p>
<p>H2O. This same behavior was also observed in 74 
</p>
<p>microgravity droplet experiments [16] and is caused 75 
</p>
<p>by H2O radiance increasing as pressure increases 76 
</p>
<p>while CO2 radiance remains nearly constant. 77

<p>4. Conclusions 26 
</p>
<p>The effects that ambient pressure had on radiance 27 
</p>
<p>in simulations of microgravity burner-supported 28 
</p>
<p>spherical diffusion flames were presented and 29 
</p>
<p>compared to the results of microgravity droplet 30 
</p>
<p>combustion experiments. Hot, warm, and cool flames 31 
</p>
<p>were all considered. The main effect of increasing 32 
</p>
<p>pressure was an increase in the reabsorption of 33 
</p>
<p>radiation, particularly for CO2, due to increased 34 
</p>
<p>absorption coefficients. The main radiators 35 
</p>
<p>throughout all three combustion regimes were CO2 36 
</p>
<p>and H2O, with CO never constituting more than 15% 37 
</p>
<p>of the total radiance.  38 
</p>
<p>The contribution to total radiance shifted from CO2 39 
</p>
<p>dominated to H2O dominated as the flame burned and 40 
</p>
<p>as pressure increased in the hot flame. In the warm 41 
</p>
<p>and cool flames H2O continued to contribute the 42 
</p>
<p>majority of the radiance, and this contribution 43 
</p>
<p>increased with increasing pressure. These trends, 44 
</p>
<p>which were also observed experimentally, were not 45 
</p>
<p>present when optically thin calculations were 46 
</p>
<p>performed, i.e., when the radiation reabsorption term 47 
</p>
<p>was removed from the radiative transfer equation.  48 
</p>
<p>This shift in radiation contributions was 49 
</p>
<p>determined to be caused by the optically thick nature 50 
</p>
<p>of the system. As absorption coefficient increased due 51 
</p>
<p>to increased product concentrations, the CO2 52 
</p>
<p>absorption band centered at 4.3 µm became easily 53 
</p>
<p>saturated. This meant CO2 radiance saw much lower 54 
</p>
<p>increases as absorption coefficient increased 55 
</p>
<p>compared to H2O radiance. This result reinforces the 56 
</p>
<p>notion that optically thin radiation models are unable 57 
</p>
<p>to accurately capture the radiation behavior of high-58 
</p>
<p>pressure combustion systems. 59
