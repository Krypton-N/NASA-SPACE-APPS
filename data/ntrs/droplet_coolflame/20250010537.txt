<p>Objective
</p>
<p>Previous student design challenge
Goal: Build a device to passively eject droplets out of a tube 
in microgravity using household fluids
Past Results: Little to no success in being able to recreate 
spontaneous capillarity driven droplet auto ejection

(Wollman 2012)

2. Build a testing apparatus that could be used to support 
</p>

3. Predict if auto ejection will occur based on fluidic

<p>Final Thoughts
</p>
<p>TakeawaysFindings
• Capillary rise with water is very hard 
</p>
<p>to achieve due to poor wetting.
</p>
<p>• Capillary rise with mineral oil is much 
</p>
<p>easier but more viscous.
</p>
<p>• Auto ejection was possible with water 
</p>
<p>with dish soap because of its low 
</p>
<p>viscosity and good wetting.
</p>
<p>• Model prediction were off because of 
</p>
<p>some major assumptions to allow for 
</p>
<p>a simpler model.
</p>
<p>– Assumed perfect wetting
</p>
<p>– Neglected entrance region loss
</p>
<p>• Many of the tubes didn’t have short 
</p>
<p>enough nozzles for the mineral oil 
</p>
<p>and explains tube 36.
</p>
<p>• Auto ejection is repeatable and 
</p>
<p>predictable under the right conditions.
</p>
<p>• Auto ejection is helpful for 
</p>
<p>understanding fluid dynamics in 
</p>
<p>microgravity environments.
</p>
<p>• Can be used as a student design 
</p>
<p>challenge for STEM educational 
</p>
<p>experiments.
</p>
<p>• Alternative passive droplet delivery 
</p>
<p>system that could be used for 
</p>
<p>different microgravity experiments.
</p>
<p>– Combustion 
</p>
<p>– Drop formation 
</p>
<p>– Jet breakup
</p>
<p>– Drop impact</p>
