<p>Spacecraft are designed to prevent fires through careful material selection and 
configuration control combined with electrical design controls and isolation of ignition 
sources. Despite these best efforts, fire risk still exists due to the potential for configuration 
changes with the introduction of crew and the potential for a ‘failure of imagination’ by 
designers in anticipating all fuel and ignition sources. Therefore, fire suppression is a critical 
capability for any crewed spacecraft as a last resort for crew survival. Many spacecraft 
suppression methods have relied on fire suppressants such as compressed carbon dioxide, 
nitrogen, and water. A proposed alternative is depressurization of the cabin as a method to 
extinguish a fire. Limited testing was conducted during Skylab and showed that depress could 
result in stirring the air flow and aggravating a fire before extinguishing. However, limited 
information was available on how the test was conducted. To better characterize the approach, 
testing was conducted at NASA’s White Sands Test Facility to evaluate the effect of 
depressurization on material extinguishment. The results showed that depressurization does 
not provide a safe and effective fire suppression capability. The testing approach, results, and 
conclusions are described and discussed.   
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
PACE vehicle designs typically prevent fires through careful material selection, ignition source isolation, and 
providing effective fire detection and suppression. Designing for fire control in addition to designing for 
</p>
<p>prevention is considered a baseline in human-rated spacecraft because there is always residual risk of a fire. Gateway 
requirements, as documented in GP10004 Gateway Program Subsystem Specification for the Environmental Control 
and Life Support System (ECLSS) allow taking exception to fixed fire suppression and detection if an alternate 
depressurization approach is implemented as outlined in GP 10004 ECLSS-BP-0002 Fire Suppression. This approach 
describes a venting implementation in which the cabin is backfilled with nitrogen to lower oxygen concentration below 
flammability levels. This approach involves venting as a means of fire suppression, therefore not requiring a fixed fire 
suppression system.  
 Current ECLSS-BP-0002 requirements specify that fire mitigation response should extinguish a fire within 
minutes by depressurizing the vehicle to 4 psia (27.6 kPa) to reduce the oxygen partial pressure prior to backfilling 
with nitrogen gas. Venting performance reported by module designers shows it would take an order of magnitude 
more time to vent the cabin to 4 psia. This is more representative of a fire response using depressurization only, rather 
than the recommended depressurization along with nitrogen backfill. Due to limited historical test data on the 
effectiveness of depressurization as a mitigation strategy for fire suppression evaluated during the Skylab program, a 
need was seen by NASA Johnson Space Center Gateway Materials &amp; Processes (M&amp;P) to test material flammability 
response to fire suppression by depressurization in the Gateway 30% maximum expected oxygen environment. 
</p>
<p>II. Background 
Despite being commonly listed as the last resort fire suppression approach, fire extinguishment by cabin venting 
</p>
<p>has received very little study. This is partly because the process takes longer than any ground-based or suborbital 
facility can offer due to the real challenges raised by any system to provide rapid venting. Challenges  posed by venting 
include: air movement induced by the venting process can increase fire damage until fire extinguishment is achieved; 
hardware and systems need to be certified to survive vacuum; crew evacuation is required; potential exterior damage 
to the spacecraft from evacuated particulate; risk of foreign object damage from the venting process to the vent valve 
(critical to spacecraft integrity); weight and volume penalty of  the large valve needed to vent quickly; and in vacuum, 
materials will cool slowly so reentry may be delayed.  
</p>
<p>The early work of Kimzey on Skylab [1] and [2],  included testing venting as a means of extinguishment of several 
materials. Six different materials (aluminized mylar, nylon, neoprene coated nylon fabric, polyurethane foam, paper 
and Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)) were tested in multiple configurations. For each material, one test attempted to 
extinguish the sample by venting the chamber. The documented results identify that paper, neoprene-coated nylon 
fabric, and polyurethane foam samples all flared up significantly. Other tests performed were considered invalid as 
the PTFE and nylon samples had ignition difficulties and the mylar sample may not have been vented. The tests were 
conducted in a 41 liter chamber in the Skylab atmosphere of 65% oxygen and 5.2 psia (35.9 kPa). There is no record 
of the venting rate used in the evaluation or the pressure where extinguishment was achieved, however it was observed 
that there was a substantial difference between the slow flame spread under quiescent conditions and the rapid flame 
spread that was observed during depressurization venting.  
</p>

<p>Goldmeer [3] and Goldmeer et al. [4] 
conducted a numerical assessment combined 
with testing in 1-g and in a parabolic aircraft. 
The overall literature on the impact of pressure 
on flame spread and growth is also covered in 
this assessment. The test materials were 
Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) cylinders 
2.54 cm long and most were 1.9 cm in diameter. 
The extinguishment pressure in low gravity was 
found to be strongly influenced by the amount 
of preheating (represented by the centerline 
temperature) the sample had received. Cold 
samples extinguished more easily at pressures 
near 0.25 Atmospheres (Atm) (3.675 psia, 25.3 
kPa) but samples with more residual heat from 
a longer burn duration survived to less than 0.1 
Atm (1.47 psia, 10.03 kPa), as shown in Figure 
1. These results indicate that rapid venting is 
necessary to assure extinction. If venting is conducted more slowly, the venting process supports enhanced flame 
growth which heats the solid further, thereby lowering the extinction pressure. Goldmeer [3] also estimated the venting 
induced velocities, and for a given vehicle volume and vent design, a wide range of velocities were predicted based 
on the distance from the vent. Additional variations would also be expected due to varied cabin internal layouts and 
hardware. Programs such as Gateway have considered partial venting followed by backfill with nitrogen to produce a 
hypoxic environment (O2 concentration less than 14%) which was believed not to sustain a fire [GP 10004]. However, 
the potential advantage with this approach may not outweigh the risk since although the venting time is shortened, it 
is still significant when combined with the time it takes to backfill with nitrogen to ensure extinguishment. 
Additionally, the proposed backfill of nitrogen in [GP 10004] occurs after depressurization to 4.0 psia (27.6 kPa). 
Based on an initial atmosphere of 30% oxygen, repressurization to 10.2 psia (70.3 kPa) with nitrogen would yield 
</p>
<p>approximately 11.7% oxygen. Previously unpublished  testing 
(Table 1 was performed at White Sands Test Facility (WSTF) 
to determine how low oxygen concentrations would need to be 
to extinguish certain materials. The testing was performed 
with Beefy T cotton t-shirt material and nylon lacing tape in a 
ISO 4589-4 Test apparatus modified to operate in sub ambient 
pressures. A flame was established on the material with an 
environmental condition of 14.7 psia, 24% O2 and balance of 
nitrogen. Nitrogen was then introduced until the targeted lower 
oxygen concentration was achieved. The test was repeated 
with new samples for each target oxygen concentration until 
extinguishment was observed. Oxygen was reintroduced after 
a predetermined dwell time to understand reigntion. The test 
</p>
<p>showed that common cabin materials continue to burn down to 11% oxygen with a balance of nitrogen, meaning that 
achieving 11.7% O2  for Gateway would be at risk of not being an effective method of fire suppression even if 
implemented per the guidance provided. Other programs may propose similar mitigation strategies that involve 
depressurization, possibly combined with a nitrogen backfill, to achieve extinguishment. This study sought to expand 
on previous WSTF testing by characterizing the effects of depressurization as a fire suppression method.  
</p>
<p>III. Materials and Methods 
To better understand the behavior of a fire during depressurization, a series of tests were performed using the 
</p>
<p>NASA-STD-6001 [5] Test 1 upward propagation test configuration. Two primary test configurations were used to 
explore the differences between maximum velocity gas flow and variable cabin flow on the test sample within the 
chamber. Varying flow rates were used to understand fire behavior based on test equipment capability. The same 
material was placed at three different locations within the test chamber to demonstrate how flow rate can vary with 
distance from the depressurization valve. To standardize the test approach, the test environment was maintained at 
30% oxygen and 14.7 psia (101.35 kPa) for all tests.

<p>B. Materials 
To develop a standard 
</p>
<p>approach of testing the 
effects of depressurization 
on a fire, three materials 
were selected as outlined in 
Table 2. The first, cotton t-
shirt (Beefy-T), is a 
commonly used material that does not pass NASA-STD-6001 Test 1 requirements at 24% oxygen. Nomex was 
selected as a high performing self-extinguishing material with respect to flammability, meeting NASA-STD-6001 
Test 1 requirements at 30% in the “J” configuration. The “J” configuration is a surface ignition test where the sample 
is placed in a “J” position with the ignitor at the bottom of the curve as compared to the standard edge ignition 
configuration used for NASA-STD-6001 Test 1. This configuration is a modification conducted on the material 
surface rather than using edge ignition. For the purpose of this test, both configurations were used to ensure stable 
ignition so that the depressurization method could be assessed ABS was selected due to its strong flame characteristics 
and high blow off velocities. This material was most representative of a stable fire that would not be blown off due to 
turbulence which would allow a best assessment of the reaction time required to reach extinguishment due to oxygen 
starvation during chamber depressurization.  
</p>
<p>All three materials were first tested with no depressurization to establish the baseline behavior of each material in 
a NASA-STD-6001 test 1 configuration at 30% oxygen and 14.7 psia (101.35 kPa) so that the flammability properties 
of the material due to depressurization of the test chamber could be compared to standard flammability properties.  
</p>
<p>C. Test Methodology  
Testing for each of the configurations commenced with the evacuation of both chambers through the larger vacuum 
</p>
<p>reservoir chamber via vacuum roughing pump to approximately 0.08 psia (0.5 kPa). The vacuum pump tank isolation 
valves were then closed, and a premixed nitrox mixture of 30% oxygen was back filled into the test chamber to a 
pressure of 14.7 psia (101.35 kPa). Ignition of the test samples was initiated by applying 15 Amps to NASA-STD-
6001 ignitors, helically wrapped within 20-gauge nichrome wire. Samples were allowed to burn long enough to ensure 
full ignition of the bottom edge or across the full sample width prior to initiation of test chamber depressurization to 
maintain consistency with NASA-STD-6001 Test 1. Full ignition was achieved at different rates for each material, 
therefore all tests for a single material were given the same amount of time to minimize the variation in thermal affects 
observed by Goldmeer. Pressure within the test chamber was captured during the test via a pressure readout on the 
test video. Samples were allowed to burn until either the sample was fully consumed or extinguished. Pressure of both 
chambers and velocity measurements within the  2-inch (5 cm) vent line were collected throughout the duration of the 
test.  
</p>
<p>Two pressurized chambers were used to demonstrate the effects of depressurization on an open flame. A test 
chamber, 1.13 m3, housed test samples at an initial pressure of 14.7 psia (101.35 kPa). A larger chamber, 8.13 m3, was 
used as the vacuum reservoir for this demonstration as depicted in Figure 4. The larger chamber is the largest 
flammability chamber at WSTF and was used as a source to ensure rapid, repeatable depressurization as opposed to 
relying solely on a vacuum pump.  
</p>

<p>A Sierra Instruments Model 620S flow meter was placed between the two chambers to determine the flow velocity

to maintain consistency amongst test coupons. Three initial flow velocities were used to characterize the effects of 
different flow rates on the test sample.  
</p>
<p>D. Test Configurations 
This test incorporated two test configurations to account for different types of gas flow during vehicle 
</p>
<p>depressurization. These tests were intended to understand different responses to depressurization based on the location 
of a fire in relation to the depressurization valve.  
</p>
<p>A three-sample configuration was used to account for open gas flow within the chamber as a result of 
depressurizing the chamber volume through a single outlet. This configuration was to better understand material 
behavior in an open cabin with varying distances from the depressurization valve. 
</p>
<p>A single-sample configuration was used to isolate the test coupon and calculate the gas-flow velocity that the 
sample experienced as compared to the velocity at the depressurization valve. This was so that individual test coupon 
data could be compared to a vehicle-level Computational Fluid Dynamic (CFD) model to demonstrate the effects of 
fire extinguishment by different velocities throughout a vehicle.  
</p>
<p>1. Open Cabin Flow using Three-Sample Configuration 
To understand material behavior in an open cabin, three samples 
</p>
<p>were ignited in the same chamber at the same time to account for 
multiple locations from the outlet as the test chamber was 
depressurized. Three samples were placed in standard NASA-STD-
6001 Test 1 mounts all in the same plane as each other as well as the 
same plane as the outlet of the test chamber. The height of each 
sample was varied, as shown in Figure 5, to account for different 
positions within the chamber. The center sample was tested with the 
standard edge ignition approach whereas the two samples to the left 
and right were in the J-configuration for the cotton t-shirt and orange 
Nomex. ABS is a stiff material that cannot be formed in the J-
configuration, therefore all ABS tests were performed with edge 
ignition only.

<p>2. Chimney Flow using a Single-Sample Configuration 
Assessing depressurization flow velocity on individual samples 
</p>
<p>required a single-sample configuration where the sample was 
placed near the outlet of the test chamber. All of these tests were 
performed using edge ignition only. A polycarbonate cylinder, 
labeled chimney in Figure 6,  with a diameter of 5.4 inches (13.7 
cm) was placed around the sample to calculate the flow velocity as 
compared to the measured flow velocity in the piping between the 
two pressure chambers. This allowed for the determination of the 
initial flow velocity experienced by the sample to later compare to 
a CFD model. The intent was to demonstrate the effects of different 
velocities throughout a vehicle when depressurization is used to 
extinguish a fire. 
</p>
<p>E. Velocity 
Test velocities were limited by the ability of gas flow from the test chamber to the vacuum chamber with both 
</p>
<p>valves completely open. The outlet velocity of the vehicle depressurization valve was not achievable with the test 
equipment, but a range was tested with the intent to understand how a fire would respond to different flow velocities 
based on the position with respect to the depressurization valve. Sample velocities were calculated based on the 
configuration outlined in Figure 6. The maximum chimney velocity was calculated comparing the diameters of the 
chimney and pipe containing the flow meter to demonstrate the measured gas flow as the two chamber pressures were 
allowed to equilibrate. 
</p>
<p>Test velocities were determined by how open the metering valve was, characterized by the number of turns of the 
valve wheel handle.  A simplified test matrix is shown in Table 3, demonstrating the varying test velocities based on 
the position of the metering valve. With the metering valve completely open, the fastest velocity resulted in the test 
chamber reaching approximately 1 psia (6.9 kPa) within 15 seconds. A moderate velocity was determined with eight 
turns of the valve wheel from the shut position, resulting in the test chamber depressurizing in approximately 26 
seconds. A representative slow velocity was tested with 4 turns of the wheel from the closed position resulting in 
depressurization within approximately 2 minutes. Slower depressurization rates were explored but not used as they 
resulted in fire extinguish properties similar to baseline tests with no depressurization of the chamber. 
</p>

<p>V. Results and Discussion 
Conceptually, depressurizing a spacecraft during a fire event would be expected to extinguish the fire by either 
</p>
<p>providing a high rate of flow to ‘blow off’ the fire or by achieving sufficiently low enough pressure such that there 
would be insufficient oxygen to maintain the flame. This study evaluated the effect of depressurization on three 
materials. The effectiveness of high velocity flow was evaluated by looking at various flow rates achieved through 
varying depressurization rate, location in the test chamber, and presence or absence of a ‘chimney’ to maximize local 
gas flow. The effectiveness of minimizing pressure was assessed at various depressurization rates.   
</p>

extinguished in two of the four tests. For the 15 second (s) and 26s depress rates, the Nomex extinguished in

<p>C. Time to Depressurize 
The ‘best practice’ documented in 
</p>
<p>GP10004 ECLSS-BP-0002 was to 
depressurize the cabin to 4 psia to reduce the 
oxygen partial pressure. Accordingly, several 
tests were paused when the test chamber 
reached 4 psia (27.6 kPa) to better understand 
the capability of lowered oxygen partial 
pressure to extinguish the flame. Several test 
samples maintained a flame down to 4 psia 
and when the vent valve was opened to 
continue depressurization of the test chamber, 
the fire was re-ignited. As discussed in 
Section B, materials continued to burn near 
and below 1 psia (6.9 kPa) for both the cotton 
t-shirt and ABS test samples. This 
demonstrated that the combination of 
depressurization blow-off and reduction of oxygen partial pressure were both insufficient to extinguish the fire. 
</p>
<p>In the time required to depressurize the test chamber volume (1.13 m3) to 4 psia (27.6 kPa) with a moderate 
depressurization rate of approximately 2 minutes, a fire was active during the entire depressurization event for most 
samples. When extrapolated to larger vehicle volumes, the time to depressurize the volume increases, resulting in the 
potential for a fire to spread when relying on depressurization as a fire extinguish method alone. Reported module 
depressurization rates based on vent valve capability show that the length of time a larger vehicle would take to 
depressurize to 4 psia is an order of magnitude greater than the pressure chamber used for these tests. For a vehicle up 
to 30 cubic meters in size it would take up to 25 minutes to depressurize to 4 psia where it was previously believed 
the oxygen partial pressure was low enough to extinguish a fire. As these tests have shown, this assumption is 
insufficient to extinguish a fire. Solely dropping the pressure cannot be expected to extinguish a fire. Even if 
backfilling with nitrogen, the vehicle would need to be depressurized to an intermediate pressure followed by 
backfilling with nitrogen to achieve an oxygen concentration below 11%. A fire would be sustained for the duration 
it would take to depressurize the vehicle and repressurize with nitrogen. Additionally, each cycle would exacerbate 
the flames as noted in this test with changes in gas flow rates. 
</p>

<p>VI. Conclusion and Future Work 
Testing of Nomex showed that sufficiently high velocity can blow off flames. However, any real spacecraft will 
</p>
<p>contain a complex arrangement of objects, all of which can create wake zones and induce eddies with the result that 
the potential velocity ranges from the peak at the outlet to the valve to any value down to zero. As a result, it is not 
reasonable to assume that it will be possible to induce flame blow off by the vent flow. Not only will areas with 
insufficient velocity flow not extinguish the flame, but the data shows that flames will be exacerbated, risking spread 
to a greater volume. Additionally, for materials that do achieve blow off during depress, residual heat remaining in 
the material can cause the material to reignite. Testing of cotton t-shirt and 0.065” ABS showed that achieving low 
pressure is not an effective way to extinguish a fire. Material can continue to burn at less than 1 psia for extended 
durations (on the order of minutes based on cotton and ABS). Based on this data, depressurization as a method of fire 
suppression in spacecraft is highly discouraged.  
</p>
<p>Future work will involve CFD development to assess local and bulk flow velocity in a representative spacecraft.
